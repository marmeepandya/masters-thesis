\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

Modern company intelligence platforms maintain datasets containing tens of millions of organisation profiles, covering everything from small local businesses to large multinational enterprises. Despite the availability of this data, finding the companies that are actually relevant to a given search intent remains difficult in practice. Existing workflows typically rely on manually configured keyword filters, which require the user to guess the exact vocabulary a relevant company happens to use in its own description, and which need multiple rounds of trial and error before returning a satisfactory set of results. This is a version of a well-known limitation of classical keyword-based retrieval: two texts can describe the same underlying concept using entirely different words, so a company that is a strong match for a query may never be surfaced simply because it does not happen to repeat the query's own terminology.

At the same time, any system intended to operate on a company database of this size must satisfy strict constraints on efficiency and scalability. Retrieval has to remain accurate while operating over a large-scale, high-cardinality dataset, and it has to return results quickly enough to support an interactive search experience, not just a slow, offline batch process.

This thesis investigates whether embedding-based semantic retrieval, where both queries and companies are represented as dense vectors and matched by meaning rather than by literal word overlap, can address this gap. Concretely, the thesis asks: can embedding-based semantic retrieval, combined with approximate nearest-neighbour search, efficiently generate accurate top-$k$ candidate company sets from a large-scale company dataset, while maintaining low query latency, and how does it compare to the keyword-based production system currently in use?

This work is conducted in collaboration with ISTARI.AI, a data intelligence company whose core product is a curated, organisation-level dataset covering companies worldwide. The collaboration grounds the thesis in a real operational setting: the queries evaluated throughout this thesis are real search intents drawn from Istari's own usage, and the production system this thesis compares against is the system Istari's users interact with today, rather than a synthetic or purely academic benchmark.

\paragraph{Contributions.} This thesis makes the following contributions. First, it presents a systematic, large-scale comparison of retrieval approaches for company search, spanning classical lexical retrieval, more than ten dense embedding models of varying size and training objective, a late-interaction retrieval architecture, and a retrieval-specific pre-trained encoder, all evaluated on the same corpus, query set, and metrics, so that results are directly comparable across methods. Second, it develops and evaluates a series of hybrid retrieval pipelines, culminating in a learned fusion ranker that is trained directly on this task's own queries and outperforms every pipeline built purely from frozen, pretrained components, while also yielding an interpretable account of which retrieval signals actually drive relevance. Third, it evaluates the accuracy-efficiency trade-off of approximate nearest-neighbour search at simulated production scale, and provides a decomposed latency analysis that separates local computation from network overhead for API-based methods. Fourth, it identifies, quantifies, and discusses a previously unquantified data-quality issue in the underlying corpus, in which a meaningful share of AI-generated company summaries describe a website hosting or platform provider rather than the company itself, and shows both how much this affects retrieval and why it was not corrected in the results reported here. Finally, because no human-annotated relevance judgements exist for this task, the thesis is explicit throughout about the limitations of the pseudo-relevance evaluation protocol it adopts instead, and includes an independent validation of that protocol against large language model-based relevance judgements.

\paragraph{Thesis structure.} Chapter~\ref{ch:istariAndData} introduces Istari's Global Organisation Index, the dataset underlying every experiment in this thesis, describing how it is constructed, its schema, and the evaluation corpus derived from it. Chapter~\ref{ch:related_literature} reviews the retrieval paradigms, indexing techniques, and evaluation practices that this thesis builds on and compares against, including sparse and dense retrieval, approximate nearest-neighbour search, late-interaction retrieval, hybrid retrieval and learning to rank, and evaluation practice in the absence of human relevance judgements. Chapter~\ref{ch:experiments} presents the experimental work itself: the baseline retrieval systems and evaluation protocol, an extended comparison across many additional embedding models, several hybrid and query-expansion strategies, an evaluation-methodology check against large language model-based judgements, a comparison against Istari's own production search APIs, an approximate nearest-neighbour scaling experiment, a detailed error analysis, the corpus data-quality finding concerning AI-generated summaries, and finally the learned fusion ranker that achieves the best result obtained anywhere in this thesis.

\section{About ISTARI.AI}

This thesis is conducted in collaboration with ISTARI.AI\protect\footnote{\url{https://docs.istari.ai/goi/goi_start_page/}}, a data intelligence company that specialises in providing high-quality firmographic data at scale. Istari's core product is a curated, organisation-level dataset that covers organisations worldwide, enabling use cases such as market analysis, company search, and lead generation. The central research challenge addressed in this thesis is improving the quality of semantic company retrieval, arises directly from Istari's operational needs.

\section{The Global Organisation Index (GOI)}

The primary dataset used in this thesis is Istari's \textbf{Global Organisation Index} (GOI), a curated collection of approximately 20 million active organisations spanning 232 countries and territories. The GOI serves as the canonical source of firmographic data within Istari's data infrastructure, covering organisation identity, location, industry classification, size, etc.

\section{Data Pipeline and Methodology}

The GOI is constructed through a multi-stage validation pipeline that deliberately prioritises data quality over volume. The pipeline proceeds as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Registry Collection.} National registers are systematically queried worldwide, including commercial registers, company registers, and association registers. These primary sources are enriched with additional open data sources, yielding an initial pool of approximately 400 million organisations.

    \item \textbf{Domain Attribution.} Each organisation is assessed for attribution to a clearly identifiable web domain. Approximately 10\% of the initial pool, around 40 million organisations can be matched to a domain. The remainder are excluded as they are either no longer active, were never truly operational (e.g., pure holding structures), or lack a web presence.

    \item \textbf{Activity Verification.} The 40 million domain-attributed organisations are further filtered by verifying that their domains are still actively operated. This final step reduces the dataset to approximately 20 million organisations that are demonstrably active.
\end{enumerate}

This pipeline ensures that every record in the GOI is both web domain-verified and confirmed active, a key differentiator from traditional firmographic databases, which typically retain large numbers of inactive or dormant records.

\section{Sparse and Dense Retrieval}
\label{sec:lit_sparse_dense}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Text retrieval systems fall into two broad families. \textbf{Sparse}
retrieval represents queries and documents as high-dimensional vectors
of term weights and scores matches by term overlap. The dominant sparse
method is BM25 \citep{robertson2009probabilistic}, a probabilistic
scoring function that weighs each query term by its frequency in a
document and its rarity across the whole collection. BM25 is fast,
requires no training, and remains a strong baseline decades after its
introduction, but it cannot match a query and a document that describe
the same concept using different words: this is the vocabulary mismatch
problem, directly observed for this thesis's task in
Section~\ref{subsec:bm25_qual}.

\textbf{Dense} retrieval instead represents queries and documents as
fixed-size vectors learned by a neural encoder, and scores matches by
vector similarity rather than literal term overlap. Dense Passage
Retrieval (DPR) \citep{karpukhin2020dense} established the now-standard
bi-encoder architecture for open-domain retrieval: a query encoder and a
document (or, in this thesis, company) encoder, trained so that relevant
pairs are close in vector space, encoding each side independently so
that documents can be embedded once, offline, and reused across every
future query. Sentence-BERT \citep{reimers2019sentence} showed that the
same bi-encoder idea, applied to general-purpose sentence embeddings
rather than a retrieval-specific training objective, produces embeddings
useful for semantic similarity and retrieval tasks broadly, and underlies
the general-purpose sentence-transformer models (including MiniLM,
Section~\ref{subsec:minilm}) used throughout this thesis. More recent
embedding models, such as the BGE family \citep{xiao2024cpack,chen2024bge}, combine
this bi-encoder architecture with large-scale contrastive pretraining and
hard-negative mining, and represent the current state of the art for
general-purpose dense retrieval, forming the basis of several baselines
evaluated in Sections~\ref{subsec:bge} and~\ref{sec:extended_baselines}.

\section{Approximate Nearest Neighbour Search}
\label{sec:lit_ann}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Dense retrieval requires finding the nearest vectors to a query vector
among the full document collection. Exact search costs time linear in
the number of documents, which becomes impractical at the scale of
millions of documents relevant to this thesis's target deployment
(Section~\ref{ch:istariAndData}). Approximate nearest neighbour (ANN)
search trades a small amount of accuracy for a large reduction in query
time by searching only a carefully chosen subset of the collection
instead of comparing the query against every document. FAISS \citep{johnson2017billion}
provides GPU-accelerated implementations of both exact and approximate
search, including inverted-file indexes (IVF), which cluster the
embedding space and search only the clusters nearest the query, trading
a small amount of recall for a large reduction in query time. Hierarchical
Navigable Small World graphs (HNSW) \citep{malkov2020efficient} take a
different approach: they build a multi-layer proximity graph over the
document vectors and greedily traverse it at query time, again trading
exactness for speed. Both IVF and HNSW are evaluated directly in this
thesis's ANN scaling experiment (Section~\ref{sec:ann_scaling}), which
tests whether the accuracy--speed trade-off these methods make remains
favourable at simulated production scale.

% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Late Interaction and Retrieval-Specific Pretraining}
\label{sec:lit_late_interaction}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

A bi-encoder compresses an entire document into one fixed-size vector,
which can discard fine-grained information relevant to a specific query.
ColBERT \citep{khattab2020colbert} addresses this with \emph{late
interaction}: query and document are each represented as a set of
per-token vectors, and relevance is scored by summing, for every query
token, the maximum similarity to any document token (MaxSim), so no
single vector has to summarise the whole document in advance. This adds
compute and storage cost relative to a single-vector bi-encoder, since
retrieval now requires comparing token sets rather than single vectors.
ColBERTv2 \citep{santhanam2022colbertv2} introduces residual compression
of the per-token vectors and the PLAID engine for efficient late-interaction
search at scale, substantially reducing this cost; this thesis instead
implements MaxSim scoring directly (Section~\ref{sec:colbert}), trading
the PLAID engine's efficiency for an exact, dependency-light
implementation suited to the scale of the evaluation corpus used here.

A separate line of work improves dense retrieval not through
architecture but through pretraining. SimLM \citep{wang2022simlm}
pretrains a bi-encoder with a representation-bottleneck objective before
any retrieval-specific fine-tuning, explicitly designed to produce
embeddings well suited to downstream passage retrieval. This thesis
evaluates whether that retrieval-specific pretraining transfers to a
domain (company profiles) quite different from the passage-retrieval
benchmarks it was designed for (Section~\ref{sec:simlm}).

\section{Hybrid Retrieval, Reranking, and Learning to Rank}
\label{sec:lit_hybrid}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Sparse and dense retrieval, and different dense models, make different
errors: a hybrid system that combines multiple retrieval signals can
recover from a single method's individual weaknesses. Reciprocal Rank
Fusion (RRF) \citep{cormack2009reciprocal} is a simple, training-free way
to combine several ranked lists: each document's fused score is the sum
of $1/(k + \mathrm{rank})$ across the lists it appears in, requiring only
that a document be ranked, not that its raw scores be comparable across
methods. RRF underlies the hybrid baseline in Section~\ref{sec:rrf}.

A complementary technique is \emph{reranking}: a first-stage retriever
returns a candidate set cheaply, and a more expensive model reorders it.
Cross-encoder rerankers, such as the \texttt{bge-reranker-v2-m3}
\citep{baaireranker2024} and \texttt{mxbai-rerank-large-v1}
\citep{mixedbread2024mxbai} models used in
Sections~\ref{sec:hybrid_fullpool} and~\ref{sec:reranker_ablation}, feed
the query and each candidate into the same transformer jointly, rather
than encoding them independently as a bi-encoder does. This lets the
model attend directly between query and candidate tokens, typically
producing more accurate relevance scores than a bi-encoder, at the cost
of requiring a full forward pass per candidate rather than a single fast
vector comparison, so cross-encoders are used to rerank a small candidate
set rather than to search a full collection.

More generally, combining multiple retrieval signals into a single
relevance score can be framed as \emph{learning to rank}
\citep{liu2009learning}: instead of a fixed, hand-designed combination
rule such as RRF, a model is trained directly on labelled query-document
pairs to learn how to weight and combine whatever features are available
(similarity scores, ranks, or other signals) into a final ranking. This
is the approach taken by the learned fusion ranker in
Section~\ref{sec:learned_fusion}, implemented with the logistic
regression and gradient-boosted tree models provided by scikit-learn
\citep{pedregosa2011scikit}.

\section{Evaluation Without Human Relevance Judgements}
\label{sec:lit_evaluation}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Standard information retrieval evaluation compares each system's
ranking against a set of human relevance judgements for a fixed query
set, historically constructed through pooling: the top results from
several systems are merged and judged by human assessors, as established
by the methodology of the Text REtrieval Conference (TREC)
\citep{voorhees1999trec}. No such
human-annotated relevance judgements exist for the company search task
addressed in this thesis. In their absence, this thesis instead adopts a
\emph{pseudo-relevance} protocol (Section~\ref{subsec:eval_protocol}):
a company is treated as relevant if it appears in the existing production
system's own results for that query. This is a common practice when
human judgements are unavailable, and the BEIR benchmark
\citep{thakur2021beir} similarly demonstrates that retrieval methods can
be compared consistently across many tasks and domains without
task-specific human annotation, provided the resulting scores are
interpreted as agreement with a reference system rather than as absolute
measures of relevance, a limitation this thesis states explicitly
wherever pseudo-relevance labels are used.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp_setup}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Dataset}
\label{subsec:dataset}

The evaluation corpus is derived from Istari's production search logs.
A set of 101 representative queries was provided by Istari, covering a
wide range of company search intents, including industry-specific queries
(e.g.\ \textit{``pharmaceutical companies in the US''}), geographic
queries (e.g.\ \textit{``tech startups in Berlin''}), and
functional queries (e.g.\ \textit{``B2B SaaS companies''}).
For each query, the production system returned its top-1\,000 ranked
results, yielding 101\,000 query--company pairs in total.
After de-duplication across queries, the corpus consists of
\textbf{98\,716 unique companies}, each represented by a domain name,
a company name, and an AI-generated natural-language summary of the
company's business activity.

The summary field was selected as the primary retrieval field after
an exploratory data analysis of all available GOI attributes.
It was found to be fully populated across the corpus (0\% missing values)
and to contain the richest semantic content for matching against
free-text queries, compared to structured fields such as industry codes
or founding year.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{subsec:eval_protocol}

\paragraph{Pseudo-relevance labels.}
No human-annotated relevance judgements are available for the Istari
company search task.
Following standard practice in information retrieval research when
ground-truth labels are absent~\citep{voorhees1999trec}, this thesis
adopts a \textit{pseudo-relevance} assumption: a company is considered
\textbf{relevant} for a given query if and only if it appears in the
\textbf{top-100 results} of Istari's production search system for
that query.
This yields a relevance set of at most 100 companies per query,
representing ${\sim}0.1\%$ of the total corpus.

This assumption has an important implication for the evaluation: the
metrics measure the degree to which each retrieval system agrees with
the production system, not whether it recovers objectively relevant
companies.
Consequently, a retrieval system that is strictly better than the
production system may appear to underperform because it surfaces
companies the production system missed.
This limitation is acknowledged throughout the analysis.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
Three standard information retrieval metrics are reported at cut-offs
$k \in \{10, 50, 100, 1000\}$:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Precision@$k$}: the fraction of the top-$k$ retrieved
    companies that are relevant.
    This measures user-facing quality: what a user sees on the first
    page of results.

    \item \textbf{Recall@$k$}: the fraction of all relevant companies
    that appear in the top-$k$ results.
    This measures coverage, important for downstream re-ranking
    pipelines that operate on a retrieved candidate set.
    \textbf{Recall is the primary metric in this thesis}, following the
    priority set by Istari for this task: whether the system finds the
    relevant companies matters more than the exact order they are
    ranked in. Section~\ref{sec:protocol_note} explains how Precision@$k$
    and Recall@$k$ relate to one another and should be read together.

    \item \textbf{NDCG@$k$} (Normalised Discounted Cumulative Gain):
    a ranking-aware metric that rewards systems for placing relevant
    companies higher in the list, using a logarithmic discount. NDCG@$k$
    is reported as a secondary, supporting metric: it reflects the
    experience of a user who interacts primarily with the top results,
    which is a reasonable concern in practice even though it is not the
    priority metric for this thesis.
\end{itemize}

All metrics are averaged across all 101 queries.
Precision and Recall are always reported at the same cut-off values
so that the precision--recall trade-off at each depth is visible.

\subsection{Computational Environment}
\label{subsec:compute}

All experiments are conducted on the University of Mannheim's
high-performance computing (HPC) cluster.
Dense model encoding is performed on an NVIDIA A100 graphics processing
unit (GPU) with 80\,GB HBM2 memory.
BM25 retrieval runs on the central processing unit (CPU) instead, since
it does not involve any neural network computation.
Query latency measurements are performed on the same hardware and reported
as the mean over 20 benchmark queries to obtain stable estimates.
Latency is decomposed into three components (local query encoding,
FAISS index search, and external network time) so that self-hosted and
API-based methods are compared on equal terms.

\section{Sparse Retrieval Baseline: BM25}
\label{sec:baseline_sparse}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Implementation}
\label{subsec:bm25_impl}

The BM25 baseline is implemented using the \texttt{rank\_bm25} Python
library~\citep{robertson2009probabilistic}, which provides the Okapi
BM25 variant with default hyperparameters $k_1 = 1.5$ and $b = 0.75$.
All 98\,716 company summaries are tokenised using the punkt tokeniser
from the Natural Language Toolkit (NLTK)~\citep{nltk} with lower-casing
applied, and the resulting
token lists are used to construct the BM25 inverted index.
At query time, each query is tokenised identically and scored against
the index.
The top-1\,000 companies by BM25 score are retrieved for each query
and stored for evaluation.
Index construction takes 28.7 seconds and requires no GPU.

\subsection{Qualitative Analysis}
\label{subsec:bm25_qual}

Manual inspection of BM25 results reveals a consistent failure mode
arising from the \textit{vocabulary mismatch problem}: BM25 retrieves
companies that contain query keywords in their description, regardless
of whether those companies operate in the queried domain.

A representative example is the query \textit{``software companies''}.
The top BM25 results are predominantly investment banks and venture
capital firms whose summaries frequently mention the word \textit{software}
in the context of describing their investment portfolio
(e.g.\ \textit{``we invest in software, fintech, and SaaS businesses''}).
These companies are not software companies: they fund them.
BM25 cannot distinguish between a company \textit{operating} in a
domain and a company \textit{discussing} that domain.
This limitation provides a clear and concrete motivation for
semantic retrieval methods that operate on the meaning of text
rather than on token overlap.

% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Dense Retrieval Baselines}
\label{sec:baseline_dense}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Three dense retrieval baselines are evaluated, covering a range of
model sizes, computational requirements, and deployment constraints.
All three follow the same two-stage pipeline introduced by
\citet{karpukhin2020dense}: (i) encode all company summaries offline
into a dense vector index using FAISS~\citep{johnson2017billion},
and (ii) at query time, encode the query into a vector and retrieve
the top-1\,000 nearest neighbours from the index.

\subsection{all-MiniLM-L6-v2 (MiniLM)}
\label{subsec:minilm}

\texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} is a lightweight sentence-transformer
model~\citep{reimers2019sentence} with 22 million parameters,
producing 384-dimensional embeddings.
It is distilled from a larger BERT model and fine-tuned on sentence
similarity tasks.
All 98\,716 company summaries are encoded in a single batch pass,
taking 0.6 minutes on the A100 GPU.
A \texttt{faiss.IndexFlatL2} index (exact Euclidean search) is
constructed over the resulting embeddings in 0.2 seconds.
MiniLM does not require embedding normalisation.

MiniLM is included as the smallest and most computationally efficient
dense baseline, providing a lower bound on dense retrieval quality
and establishing whether a minimal dense model is sufficient to
outperform BM25.

\subsection{BGE-large-en-v1.5 (BGE)}
\label{subsec:bge}

\texttt{BAAI/bge-large-en-v1.5} is a 335-million parameter BERT-large
model developed by the Beijing Academy of Artificial
Intelligence~\citep{xiao2024cpack}, producing 1\,024-dimensional embeddings.
It is specifically optimised for dense retrieval through hard negative
mining during fine-tuning, a training strategy in which the model
is exposed to query--document pairs that are superficially similar
but semantically distinct, forcing it to learn fine-grained semantic
distinctions.

Encoding all 98\,716 company summaries takes 7.0 minutes on the A100 GPU
at a batch size of 256.
BGE requires L2 normalisation of all embeddings
(\texttt{normalize\_embeddings=True}), as it is trained under the
cosine similarity objective.
Accordingly, a \texttt{faiss.IndexFlatIP} index (inner product,
equivalent to cosine similarity for unit-normalised vectors) is used
rather than \texttt{IndexFlatL2}.

At query time, each query is encoded independently and normalised
before being passed to the FAISS index.
The query encoding step takes 16.6\,ms on the A100 GPU, and the
subsequent FAISS search takes 27.8\,ms, for a total query latency
of 44.4\,ms.

\subsection{OpenAI text-embedding-3-small (OpenAI)}
\label{subsec:openai}

\texttt{text-embedding-3-small} is a commercial embedding model
provided by OpenAI via their REST API.
It produces 1\,536-dimensional embeddings.
Unlike the open-source models above, the architecture and training
procedure are proprietary.
Encoding all 98\,716 company summaries is performed via batched API
calls (batch size 500) and takes 9.7 minutes, of which the majority
is network latency rather than model computation.
The resulting embeddings are unit-normalised by the API, and a
\texttt{faiss.IndexFlatIP} index is constructed locally.
The total cost of encoding the full corpus is approximately \$0.15
at the published pricing of \$0.02 per million tokens.

This model is included as the commercial upper-bound baseline,
following a suggestion from the industry supervisor that a
high-quality API-based embedding model be evaluated alongside
open-source alternatives.

\paragraph{Latency note.}
Query latency for the OpenAI baseline comprises two components:
a network round-trip to the OpenAI API servers (211.7\,ms, measured
over 20 queries) and a local FAISS search (42.9\,ms).
The network component is not a property of the model itself;
it reflects internet latency between the University of Mannheim cluster
and OpenAI's infrastructure, and should not be interpreted as a
measure of model speed.
For a fair comparison, the local FAISS search time (42.9\,ms)
is comparable to BGE's search time (27.8\,ms), confirming that
both methods perform equivalently at the index search stage.
The substantive difference is that OpenAI requires an external
API call for query encoding, whereas BGE encodes locally on GPU
in 16.6\,ms.

\paragraph{BM25 vs dense retrieval.}
All three dense methods outperform BM25 at low cut-offs.
At $k=10$, BGE and OpenAI achieve an NDCG of 0.227, representing a
\textbf{37\% improvement} over BM25's 0.166.
Precision@10 improves from 0.156 (BM25) to 0.216 (BGE), a 38\%
relative gain.
This confirms the hypothesis that semantic retrieval better captures
the intent of natural-language company search queries, particularly
for cases where relevant companies use vocabulary different from the
query terms.

At large cut-offs ($k = 1000$), the gap narrows.
BM25 achieves a Recall@1000 of 0.682, compared to BGE's 0.796 and
OpenAI's 0.803.
This pattern is consistent with findings in the BEIR
benchmark~\citep{thakur2021beir}: dense models are stronger at
ranking precision, while keyword-based methods maintain competitive
coverage at large retrieval depths due to their broad lexical matching.

\paragraph{BGE vs OpenAI.}
BGE and OpenAI are \textbf{effectively equivalent} across all metrics
and cut-offs.
The largest observed difference is 0.013 NDCG points (at $k=50$),
which is within the range of query-level variance and should not be
interpreted as a meaningful quality advantage.
This finding has an important practical implication: a free,
self-hosted model (BGE) achieves the same retrieval quality as a
commercial API (OpenAI) on this task.

\paragraph{MiniLM.}
MiniLM consistently underperforms BGE and OpenAI at all cut-offs,
and is only marginally better than BM25 at large $k$
(Recall@1000: 0.653 vs 0.682 for BM25).
It is also slower than BGE in production (query latency 80.9\,ms vs
44.4\,ms), as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:exp_runtime}.
MiniLM is therefore dominated by BGE on both quality and speed,
and there is no operating point at which it represents the optimal
choice.

\paragraph{Precision and the low-prevalence setting.}
Absolute precision values are low across all methods
(Precision@10 ranging from 0.156 to 0.224).
This is expected: relevant companies represent at most 100 out of
98\,716 corpus entries, approximately 0.1\% of the corpus.
In such a low-prevalence setting, even a perfect retrieval system
would show low absolute precision at large $k$.
What matters is the relative improvement between methods: BGE and
OpenAI achieve 38--43\% higher Precision@10 than BM25, which is a
meaningful gain.

\paragraph{The NDCG dip at $k = 100$.}
An artifact of the evaluation design is visible in
Table~\ref{tab:main_results}: NDCG values decrease from $k=10$
to $k=100$ before rising sharply at $k=1000$ for all methods.
This occurs because the pseudo-relevance labels are defined as the
production top-100.
At exactly $k=100$, NDCG measures whether all 100 relevant companies
are ranked perfectly within the top-100 retrieved results, a maximally
strict condition.
At $k=1000$, there is substantially more room to accommodate the relevant
set, and the metric rises accordingly.
This artifact would not appear with human-annotated relevance labels
that include varying degrees of relevance beyond a hard top-100 cut-off.

\section{Evaluation Protocol Change for Follow-Up Experiments}
\label{sec:protocol_note}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

All experiments described in the remainder of this chapter
(Sections~\ref{sec:extended_baselines}--\ref{sec:oracle_union}) use a
\textbf{relaxed pseudo-relevance definition}: a company counts as
relevant if it appears anywhere in production's \textbf{top-1\,000}
results for a query, rather than the top-100 threshold used in
Section~\ref{subsec:eval_protocol} and Table~\ref{tab:main_results}.
The relaxed threshold was adopted so that reranking, fusion, and
oracle-ceiling experiments would have enough relevant companies per
query to produce meaningful comparisons over large candidate pools.
As a direct consequence, the absolute NDCG/Precision/Recall values
reported from here on are on a different (generally much higher)
numerical scale than Table~\ref{tab:main_results}, and
\textbf{are not directly comparable to it}.
All comparisons in the following sections should be read only
relative to one another, within the same section.

\paragraph{Reading Precision and Recall together.}
Under this relaxed protocol, every one of the 101 queries has exactly
1\,000 relevant companies. This has a direct consequence for how
Precision@$k$ and Recall@$k$ relate to one another at any cut-off
$k \le 1\,000$: since both metrics are computed from the same count
(how many of the top-$k$ retrieved companies are relevant), just divided
by a different denominator,
\[
  \text{Recall@}k = \text{Precision@}k \times \frac{k}{1\,000},
\]
so the two metrics are exactly proportional to one another at every
cut-off, and neither is redundant on its own: Precision@$k$ answers
``of what the system returned, how much was correct'', while Recall@$k$
answers ``of everything that is correct, how much did the system
return'', and the two only coincide once $k = 1\,000$, where the
denominators become equal.
A practical consequence is that Recall@$k$ for small $k$ can look
misleadingly low in isolation. For example, a system that returns 10
companies of which 8 are genuinely relevant has an excellent
Precision@10 of 0.80, but the corresponding Recall@10 is only
$0.80 \times 10/1\,000 = 0.008$, not because the 10 companies returned
are poor, but because 10 items can never cover more than 1\% of a
1\,000-company relevant set. For this reason, the tables in this
chapter report Precision@$k$ at the shallow cut-offs
($k \in \{10, 50, 100, 300, 500\}$), where it is the more directly
interpretable number, and report both Precision@1\,000 and Recall@1\,000
together, since $k = 1\,000$ is the one cut-off at which Recall stops
being a rescaled copy of Precision and becomes a genuinely independent
measure: total coverage of the full relevant set. Recall@1\,000 is
treated as the headline metric throughout this thesis for exactly this
reason.

\section{BGE Instruction-Prefix Correction}
\label{sec:bge_prefix}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The original BGE-large baseline (Section~\ref{subsec:bge}) encoded
queries without an instruction prefix. BGE-large's model card
specifies that, for retrieval tasks, each \emph{query} should be
prefixed with the instruction \textit{``Represent this sentence for
searching relevant passages: ''} before encoding; the passage
(company) side requires no prefix, so the cached company embeddings
could be reused without re-encoding the corpus. This fix was retested
on both the all-fields and summary-only variants of the BGE baseline.

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\caption{Effect of the query instruction prefix on BGE-large, measured
         under the relaxed top-1\,000 protocol (Section~\ref{sec:protocol_note}).}
\label{tab:bge_prefix}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Variant} & \textbf{NDCG@10} & \textbf{Recall@1000} & $\Delta$\textbf{NDCG@10} & $\Delta$\textbf{Recall@1000} \\
\midrule
All-fields, no prefix   & 0.942 & 0.726 & --      & --      \\
All-fields, with prefix & 0.937 & 0.727 & $-$0.005 & $+$0.001 \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Finding.}
Contrary to expectation, applying the officially documented query
prefix produced only a marginal and mixed effect on this dataset:
Recall@1000 improved negligibly (+0.001 for all-fields, +0.007 for
summary-only), while NDCG@10 slightly \emph{decreased} in both variants.
Both effects are small enough to plausibly be within query-level noise.
This rules out the missing prefix as the explanation for a broader
pattern seen throughout this chapter: under the relaxed protocol, BGE
(0.726 all-fields, 0.700 to 0.707 summary-only) and the related BGE-M3
model (0.704, Section~\ref{sec:extended_baselines}) both reach a lower
Recall@1000 than the much smaller, general-purpose MiniLM model (0.741,
Table~\ref{tab:hyde}), despite BGE's larger parameter count and its
retrieval-specific fine-tuning.

\paragraph{Why a specialised, larger model can still be a worse fit.}
A plausible explanation lies in what each model was optimised for during
training, rather than in how it is queried at inference time. BGE-large
and BGE-M3 are fine-tuned through hard negative mining
(Section~\ref{subsec:bge}) on benchmark data dominated by short,
single-topic passages retrieved for open-domain question answering, an
objective that sharpens the model's embedding space around the specific
pattern of a question matched against a topically focused prose passage.
MiniLM, by contrast, is trained as a general-purpose sentence-similarity
model on a broad mixture of paraphrase and natural language inference
data \citep{reimers2019sentence}, an objective that rewards recognising
that two pieces of text mean similar things across a wide range of
domains and writing styles, rather than one narrow retrieval pattern.
The company records retrieved in this thesis are not passages: each one
concatenates heterogeneous, only loosely prose-like fields (organisation
name, country, state, city, organisation type and size, industry code)
with a short free-text summary. This structure is closer to the varied,
mixed-format text that MiniLM's training was designed to generalise
across than to the passage-retrieval benchmarks that shaped BGE's
embedding space. Fine-tuning a model tightly around one task and one
kind of text is well documented to improve performance on that specific
benchmark while sometimes reducing robustness on text the model was not
tuned for \citep{thakur2021beir}, and the results in this chapter are
consistent with that trade-off: BGE's retrieval-specific specialisation
does not transfer as cleanly to this heterogeneous company-record format
as MiniLM's broader, less specialised training does.

% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Query Expansion: HyDE Pipeline}
\label{sec:hyde}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

HyDE (Hypothetical Document Embeddings) expands each query by asking a
local LLM (\texttt{llama3.1:8b} via Ollama) to generate $N=10$
stochastic (temperature~$=0.8$) hypothetical company descriptions that
would ideally answer the query. The embeddings of these 10 descriptions
are averaged into a single query vector, which is then searched against
the MiniLM company index. A second stage reranks the top-50 HyDE
results with a cross-encoder (\texttt{BAAI/bge-reranker-v2-m3}).

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\caption{HyDE pipeline results (relaxed top-1\,000 protocol) compared
         against the plain MiniLM baseline re-evaluated under the same
         protocol.}
\label{tab:hyde}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Method} & \textbf{NDCG@10} & \textbf{Recall@1000} & \textbf{Latency (ms)} \\
\midrule
MiniLM (no expansion)        & 0.975 & 0.741 & 16.9 \\
Stage 1: HyDE + MiniLM       & 0.829 & 0.596 & 9\,246.9 \\
Stage 2: HyDE + MiniLM + Reranker & 0.954 & 0.596 & 9\,488.3 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Finding.}
Query expansion via HyDE \emph{hurts} retrieval quality relative to
directly encoding the original query: Stage 1 NDCG@10 drops from 0.975
to 0.829, and Recall@1000 drops from 0.741 to 0.596. Adding a
cross-encoder reranker (Stage 2) recovers most of the NDCG@10 loss
(0.829~$\to$~0.954) by re-ordering the top-50 candidates, but cannot
recover the recall loss, since reranking only reorders an already
-fixed candidate set. Latency is dominated overwhelmingly by the LLM
call itself (9.2 seconds), three orders of magnitude slower than
directly encoding the query with MiniLM (16.9~ms). For this dataset,
HyDE is not a worthwhile query-expansion strategy: it is far slower and
retrieves a worse candidate set than simply embedding the query
directly.

% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Hybrid Retrieval: Reciprocal Rank Fusion}
\label{sec:rrf}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

As an alternative to query expansion, BM25 and MiniLM rankings are
fused directly using Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF),
$\mathrm{RRF}(d) = \sum_{\text{method}} \frac{1}{k + \mathrm{rank}(d)}$
with $k=60$ \citep{cormack2009reciprocal}. A second stage reranks the
top-50 fused results with the same cross-encoder used for HyDE.

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\caption{Hybrid RRF results (relaxed top-1\,000 protocol).}
\label{tab:rrf}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Method} & \textbf{NDCG@10} & \textbf{Recall@1000} & \textbf{Latency (ms)} \\
\midrule
MiniLM (no fusion)              & 0.975 & 0.741 & 16.9 \\
Stage 1: RRF (BM25 + MiniLM)    & 0.955 & 0.724 & 104.9 \\
Stage 2: RRF + Reranker          & 0.975 & 0.724 & 352.0 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Finding.}
Unlike HyDE, RRF fusion of BM25 and MiniLM adds negligible latency
(the fusion step itself takes 2.2\,ms) and, after reranking, matches
the plain MiniLM baseline's NDCG@10 (0.975) while Recall@1000 remains
slightly below MiniLM alone (0.724 vs 0.741): BM25's keyword matches
do not add companies beyond what MiniLM already retrieves for this
query set, so fusion mostly re-confirms rather than extends MiniLM's
candidate pool. RRF is a much cheaper and more effective way to
incorporate a second retrieval signal than HyDE-style query expansion.

\section{Production API Evaluation and MiniLM Rerank}
\label{sec:api_eval}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Two rounds of evaluation compared the local pipeline against Istari's
live production search API, using a separate ground truth (the
production system's own graded semantic-search results at a similarity
threshold of 0.75) rather than the pseudo-relevance labels used
elsewhere in this chapter.

\paragraph{v1: keyword API.}
The first round compared Istari's v1 (BM25-based) production API
against local MiniLM, both queried against the same 101 queries.
Istari v1 BM25 NDCG@10 was 0.101 and Recall@1000 was 0.109 (network
latency 2\,828\,ms/query), against local MiniLM's NDCG@10 of 0.935 and
Recall@1000 of 0.766 (local GPU latency 26.6\,ms/query), confirming
that the production keyword API is a much weaker retrieval signal than
even the simplest local dense baseline.

\paragraph{v2: semantic API.}
A second round added Istari's newer v2 semantic search API (live,
paginated over the full 20-million-company GOI, quota-aware). Istari
v2 NDCG@10 was 0.985 and Recall@1000 (at the achievable depth of 816
results) was 0.650, clearly ahead of local MiniLM (NDCG@10 0.935,
Recall@816 0.709) at shallow cutoffs, though local MiniLM's Recall
catches up and slightly exceeds v2's at $k=816$. Network latency for
v2 (1\,966\,ms/query for a full fetch) remains far higher than local
MiniLM (26.4\,ms/query).

\paragraph{Reranking v2's own candidates with MiniLM.}
A follow-up experiment took v2's own retrieved candidate pool and
re-ordered it locally using MiniLM, to test whether local reranking
could improve on v2's native ranking without changing which companies
are retrieved. This made results \emph{worse}: NDCG@10 fell from 0.985
(v2 native ranking) to 0.855 (MiniLM-reranked). Istari's v2 semantic
ranking is not simply a MiniLM-style dense embedding search under the
hood: it outperforms MiniLM's own ranking of the same candidates.

\label{sec:ann_scaling}

To test whether the exact search used throughout this chapter

\section{Recall@1000 Error Analysis}
\label{sec:error_analysis}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

To understand why exact MiniLM search caps at Recall@1000~=~0.741
(Section~\ref{sec:ann_scaling}), every production-relevant company that
MiniLM failed to retrieve within the top-1\,000 was extracted and
searched for at much greater depth ($k=10\,000$), yielding
26\,185 missed (query, company) pairs across all 101 queries.

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering

         beyond rank 1\,000 the true company was actually ranked.}
\label{tab:error_analysis}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Bucket} & \textbf{Count} & \textbf{Share (\%)} \\
\midrule
Near miss (rank 1\,000--2\,000)   & 16\,157 & 61.7 \\

\paragraph{Finding.}
The large majority of misses (61.7\%) are near misses, just outside the

ranking granularity rather than a hard semantic failure. Only 2.3\% of
misses are not found at all within $k=10\,000$, indicating genuine

limitation, and sets a hard ceiling on achievable recall for any
embedding-based method operating on the \texttt{summary} field alone.

% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Corpus Quality: Platform Boilerplate in the AI-Generated Summary}
\label{sec:trust_filter}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The duplicated ``Bluehost.com'' description found in
Section~\ref{sec:error_analysis} is one instance of a broader,
previously unquantified data-quality issue affecting the
\texttt{summary} field that every retrieval method in this thesis
depends on. For a meaningful share of companies in the corpus, the
AI-generated summary describes the website hosting provider or
website-building platform that the company's domain happens to run on,
rather than the company's own business. This happens because many small
businesses have a thin, template-driven web presence, so the process
that generates each company's summary from its web content sometimes
picks up generic platform language (for example, marketing copy for a
web hosting provider or an e-commerce storefront builder) instead of any
information specific to the company itself. A company using such a
platform is then, in effect, indistinguishable in its summary text from
every other company using the same platform, which is precisely the
mechanism behind the large-scale description duplication already
observed in Section~\ref{sec:error_analysis}.

\paragraph{How much of the corpus is affected.}
This contamination is not spread evenly across query topics. An audit of
the pseudo-relevance labels for all 101 queries found that, for the
query \textit{``web hosting companies''}, 85.4\% of the 1\,000
ground-truth relevant companies have a boilerplate rather than a
company-specific summary; for \textit{``cloud computing providers''} the
figure is 73.1\%, and for \textit{``e-commerce platforms''} it is
53.1\%. By contrast, most other query topics in the evaluation set
(among them \textit{``software companies''}, \textit{``healthcare
providers''}, and \textit{``construction companies''}) show no measurable
contamination at all. This pattern is intuitive once the underlying
mechanism is understood: boilerplate summaries describe hosting,
domain, and e-commerce infrastructure, so they are only likely to be
retrieved, and only likely to be judged relevant by the production
system's own ranking, for queries whose intent is itself adjacent to
that infrastructure. A query about software companies is unlikely to
surface a domain registrar's boilerplate text, but a query about web
hosting companies is exactly the case in which that boilerplate text
looks, on the surface, like a plausible match.

\paragraph{An attempt to detect it automatically.}
A classifier was built to flag boilerplate summaries automatically,
using embedding similarity between each company's summary and a small,
fixed reference set of around 300 known boilerplate templates, an
approach chosen so that classifying any single company costs one
embedding comparison against a fixed-size reference set, regardless of
whether the underlying corpus is the 98\,716-company evaluation set
used in this thesis or the full multi-million-company GOI. This
classifier was validated against 300 companies independently judged by
an LLM as having a trustworthy or untrustworthy summary. At its
best-performing similarity threshold, the classifier reaches an F1
score of 0.59, with a precision of 0.52 and a recall of 0.69 for
detecting untrustworthy summaries.

\paragraph{Why this limitation was not corrected in the reported results.}
A precision of 0.52 means that of every two summaries the classifier
flags as boilerplate, roughly one is, in fact, a genuine, company-specific
summary. Removing or down-weighting every flagged company from the
retrieval pipeline would therefore discard a large number of companies
that are not actually contaminated, trading one source of error for
another rather than removing it. For this reason, no correction for
boilerplate contamination was applied to any of the results reported in
this thesis, and all recall and precision figures should be read with
this limitation in mind, particularly for the small set of query topics
identified above as heavily affected. This is reported here as an
explicitly known and quantified, but unaddressed, limitation of the
underlying corpus rather than as a solved problem: a natural direction
for future work is to use the boilerplate-similarity score not as a hard
filter but as an additional input feature to a trained model, such as
the learned fusion ranker introduced in Section~\ref{sec:learned_fusion},
which is able to learn how much to trust an imperfect signal rather than
requiring it to be reliable enough for a fixed decision rule.

% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Oracle Union Recall Ceiling}
\label{sec:oracle_union}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Before investing in a trained reranker or a fine-tuning pipeline, it is
useful to know the best-case recall achievable by simply combining the
candidate pools already produced by every method run so far --- at zero
additional encoding cost, since every channel's top-1\,000 results per
query are already cached on disk. This experiment computes, for every
combination of the six available channels (MiniLM, BM25, BGE-all-fields,
BGE-summary, OpenAI-large, Nomic), the recall of the \emph{union} of
their top-1\,000 candidate sets against the top-1\,000 ground truth.

\paragraph{Findings.}
The best single channel (MiniLM alone) reaches Recall@1000~=~0.741.
The full union of all six channels reaches 0.936 (average combined pool
size 1\,949 candidates/query), a gain of +0.196 over MiniLM alone. The
best-performing \emph{pair} is MiniLM~+~OpenAI-large, at 0.856 (average
pool size 1\,300). A pool-size sweep on this best pair shows the
ceiling is highly sensitive to how many candidates are taken per
channel: at 100 candidates/channel the union only reaches 0.150 recall,
rising to 0.581 at 500, and 0.856 at the full 1\,000.

\paragraph{Interpretation.}
Because the six-channel union clears 0.936, well above any individual
channel, the different retrieval methods are largely making
\emph{complementary}, not correlated, errors: a reranker operating over
a modestly sized union pool (rather than a single channel) is likely
sufficient to approach this ceiling, and a full fine-tuning pipeline is
not obviously necessary to close the remaining gap. The residual
0.064 recall lost even under the full six-channel union is consistent
with the hard data-quality ceiling identified in
Section~\ref{sec:error_analysis} (duplicated/boilerplate company
descriptions that no embedding-based channel can disambiguate).

% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{SimLM: A Purpose-Built Retrieval Pre-training Baseline}
\label{sec:simlm}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

All dense baselines evaluated so far are general-purpose sentence-embedding
models. \textbf{SimLM} \citep{wang2022simlm} takes a different approach:
it is a BERT-base (110\,M parameter) encoder pre-trained specifically for
passage retrieval using a representation-bottleneck objective, before any
task-specific fine-tuning. It is included to test whether a model
purpose-built for retrieval, but far smaller than the 7B-parameter models
in Section~\ref{sec:extended_baselines}, can match or exceed them.

\paragraph{Implementation.}
The public checkpoint \texttt{intfloat/simlm-base-msmarco-finetuned} is
used, following its documented convention rather than the all-fields
concatenation used elsewhere in this thesis: the company name is encoded
as a \emph{title}, and the remaining fields (country, state, city, type,
size, industry, summary, keywords) as the paired \emph{body} text, using
CLS-token pooling (not mean pooling) with L2 normalisation. Queries are
encoded without any prefix, following the same model card.

\paragraph{Finding.}
SimLM underperforms every dense baseline evaluated in this thesis, and its

a less clear-cut story here: SimLM's 0.923 sits above BM25's 0.854, since

and this task: SimLM was pre-trained and fine-tuned on MS~MARCO, a
corpus of short, single-topic passages, using a title/body sentence-pair
format that assumes the title is a concise topical label. Company names
are not topical labels in the same sense: ``Sweet Software'' or
``Springcoast Capital Partners'' convey little about what the paired body
text describes, so the sentence-pair convention that helps SimLM on
MS~MARCO-style data may provide a weaker signal here than simply
concatenating all fields into one string, as the other baselines do.
This result is a useful negative finding: retrieval-specific pre-training
does not automatically transfer across domains, and general-purpose
sentence embeddings (MiniLM, GTE-large) remain the stronger choice for
this task.

% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{ColBERT: Late-Interaction Retrieval from Scratch}
\label{sec:colbert}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Every method evaluated so far represents each company and each query as a
single dense vector. \textbf{ColBERT} \citep{khattab2020colbert,
santhanam2022colbertv2} instead represents both as \emph{sets of
per-token} embeddings and scores a (query, company) pair using
\emph{MaxSim}: for every query token, the maximum similarity to any
company token is taken, and these per-token maxima are summed. This is
architecturally richer than single-vector dense retrieval, since no
single fixed-size vector has to compress the entire meaning of a company
description, and it is included to test whether that added expressiveness
translates into higher recall on this task.

\paragraph{Implementation.}
The public checkpoint \texttt{colbert-ir/colbertv2.0} is used, encoding
each company summary into a sequence of 128-dimensional per-token
embeddings via the model's linear projection layer. Due to indexing and
library constraints on the cluster used for this thesis, the official
PLAID-indexed ColBERT pipeline was not used; instead, MaxSim scoring is
implemented from scratch as a brute-force computation over all
98\,716 companies for every query. This is computationally more expensive
than an indexed implementation would be, but is exact rather than
approximate, and is tractable at this corpus size.

a broader set of relevant companies overall but ranks the very top results

% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Hybrid Full-Pool Reranking}
\label{sec:hybrid_fullpool}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Section~\ref{sec:rrf} showed that fusing BM25 and MiniLM with Reciprocal
Rank Fusion, followed by reranking only the top-50 fused candidates,
essentially reproduces MiniLM's own quality (NDCG@10~=~0.975) without
improving Recall@1000. Section~\ref{sec:oracle_union} then showed that the
\emph{union} of several channels' top-1\,000 candidate pools reaches a
much higher oracle recall ceiling than any single channel. Together, these
two findings motivate a different hybrid design: rerank the
\emph{entire} union pool of several dense channels with a cross-encoder,
rather than fusing rankings and reranking only a shallow, fixed-size
slice of the result.

\paragraph{Implementation.}
For each query, the top-1\,000 results from a set of dense channels are
combined by simple set union (no rank fusion, no truncation), giving an
unranked candidate pool that is typically 1\,300--1\,800 companies. A
cross-encoder reranker, \texttt{BAAI/bge-reranker-v2-m3}
\citep{baaireranker2024}, then scores every (query, candidate) pair in
the full pool directly, and the top-1\,000 by reranker score are kept as
the final result. Four variants are compared, adding one channel at a
time:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{2-way}: MiniLM~+~Linq-Embed-Mistral.
    \item \textbf{3-way}: 2-way~+~GTE-large.
    \item \textbf{4-way}: 3-way~+~OpenAI-large.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Finding.}
Reranking the full union pool instead of a fixed top-50 slice raises
Recall@1000 substantially (0.782~$\to$~0.802, 2-way), at the cost of a
small drop in NDCG@10 (0.979~$\to$~0.955): the reranker now has many
more genuinely difficult candidates to rank, so its ordering of the very
top results is slightly less precise, even though it recovers more of the
true relevant set overall. Adding a third channel (GTE-large) improves
Recall@1000 further, to 0.804, the best result obtained by any hybrid
pipeline built from off-the-shelf pretrained components in this thesis.
Adding a fourth channel (OpenAI-large) does \emph{not} improve on this --
Recall@1000 is unchanged at 0.802, within noise of the 3-way result.

\paragraph{Interpretation: channel saturation.}
This flat 3-way~$\to$~4-way result is an important turning point in this
thesis's experimental narrative. Section~\ref{sec:oracle_union} showed
that combining \emph{more} candidate-generating channels reliably raises
the oracle recall ceiling; here, adding a fourth channel to an already
capable 3-way pool does not translate into a better final result. This
suggests that candidate coverage is no longer the limiting factor for this
pipeline: the union pool already contains the relevant companies needed
to reach a substantially higher recall than 0.804 (recall
Section~\ref{sec:oracle_union}'s six-channel oracle ceiling of 0.936) --
and the reranker itself is now the bottleneck preventing the pipeline from
closing that gap. This motivates two follow-up questions, addressed in the
next two sections: is the specific reranker \emph{model} the limiting
factor (Section~\ref{sec:reranker_ablation}), or is a fundamentally
different fusion approach needed (Section~\ref{sec:learned_fusion})?

% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Reranker Ablation: Isolating the Bottleneck}
\label{sec:reranker_ablation}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Given the channel-saturation finding in Section~\ref{sec:hybrid_fullpool},
the natural next question is whether the specific reranker model used
throughout this chapter, \texttt{BAAI/bge-reranker-v2-m3}, is itself
limiting recall, or whether reranker choice matters little once the
candidate pool is fixed.

\paragraph{Implementation.}
\texttt{BAAI/bge-reranker-v2-m3} (built on an XLM-RoBERTa backbone) is
replaced with \texttt{mixedbread-ai/mxbai-rerank-large-v1}
\citep{mixedbread2024mxbai} (built on a DeBERTa-v2 backbone, an

\emph{exact same} 3-way union pool from Section~\ref{sec:hybrid_fullpool}.
Only the reranker model changes; the candidate pool, corpus, and queries

% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Learned Fusion Ranker}
\label{sec:learned_fusion}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Every hybrid pipeline so far combines \emph{frozen}, independently
pretrained components: candidate lists are pooled by simple union, and a
pretrained cross-encoder, never fit to this task's own queries or
labels, reranks the result. This section instead treats fusion as a
supervised learning-to-rank problem \citep{liu2009learning}: a model is
trained directly on this thesis's own 101 queries and their pseudo-relevance
labels to learn how to combine multiple retrieval signals, rather than
relying on a fixed, hand-designed combination rule.

\paragraph{Implementation.}
For every (query, candidate) pair in the same 3-way union pool used in
Section~\ref{sec:hybrid_fullpool} (with BM25 added as a fourth,
lexical, signal), ten features are computed: the raw similarity score
and inverse rank ($1/\text{rank}$) from each of MiniLM, Linq-Embed-Mistral,
GTE-large, and BM25; the \texttt{BAAI/bge-reranker-v2-m3} score from
Section~\ref{sec:hybrid_fullpool} (included as one input feature among
ten, not as the final decision rule); and a count of how many channels
retrieved the candidate at all. Two models are trained on these features
using \texttt{scikit-learn} \citep{pedregosa2011scikit}: a logistic
regression, and a histogram-based gradient-boosted tree ensemble.
Crucially, both are trained and evaluated using 5-fold cross-validation
grouped by \texttt{query\_id}: for each fold, the model is fit on
candidates from 80\% of queries and scores held-out candidates from the
remaining 20\%, so that no query is ever scored by a model that has seen
its own label during training. Final metrics are computed by
concatenating the held-out predictions from all five folds, giving a
complete, leakage-free evaluation over all 101 queries.

\paragraph{Finding.}
The gradient-boosted learned fusion ranker reaches Recall@1000~=~0.823,
the best result obtained anywhere in this thesis, improving on the best
pretrained-component pipeline (0.804) by $+0.019$. For scale, this gain is
roughly 27 times larger than the 0.0007 movement observed when merely
swapping reranker models in Section~\ref{sec:reranker_ablation}; this is
a substantive improvement attributable to training on the task's own
data, not noise. NDCG@10 also improves (0.955~$\to$~0.979).

\paragraph{Interpretation: what the model learned.}
The trained logistic regression's coefficients offer a direct, interpretable
view into which signals matter most for this task: MiniLM's \emph{inverse
rank} has by far the largest coefficient magnitude (+5.01), exceeding
every raw similarity score \emph{and} the pretrained reranker's own score
(+0.16). In other words, where a candidate is ranked by MiniLM is a more
informative signal for final relevance than the pretrained cross-encoder's
score: a concrete, non-obvious finding that would not be visible from
simply combining components by hand. This supports the conclusion drawn
in Section~\ref{sec:reranker_ablation}: the pretrained reranker was not
extracting the maximum available signal from the candidate pool, and a
lightweight model trained on this task's own data closes a meaningful part
of the remaining gap to the oracle ceiling from Section~\ref{sec:oracle_union}.

% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Validating the Learned Fusion Ranker Against a Non-Circular Gold Standard}
\label{sec:pooled_validation}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The pseudo-relevance protocol used throughout this thesis (Section~\ref{subsec:eval_protocol}) treats a company as relevant if and only if it appears in production's own results for a query. This is a reasonable practice when no human-annotated relevance judgements exist (Section~\ref{sec:lit_evaluation}), but it carries a specific risk for the learned fusion ranker (Section~\ref{sec:learned_fusion}): a model trained directly on labels derived from production's own output may partly be learning to reproduce whatever idiosyncrasies that particular system has, rather than genuine relevance. This section reports a small-scale attempt to check whether the learned fusion ranker's key interpretive finding, that MiniLM's inverse rank is the most informative feature, survives replacing that circular ground truth with an independently constructed one.

\subsection{Building a Pooled, Independently Judged Gold Standard}
\label{subsec:pooled_gold_standard}

\paragraph{Pooling.} Following the pooling methodology established for the Text REtrieval Conference \citep{voorhees1999trec, zobel1998reliable}, the top-20 candidates per query were taken from six sources: the frozen production ground truth, a freshly fetched sample from Istari's live v2 search API, BM25, GTE-large, ColBERT, and the learned fusion ranker's own output, then unioned and deduplicated by domain. Applied across all 101 queries, this yields 8\,309 pooled candidates, an average of 82.3 unique candidates per query, a substantially smaller and more tractable set than the original 1\,000-per-query pool.

\paragraph{Trust filtering.} Before any relevance judging, every pooled candidate's summary was checked against the boilerplate detector from Section~\ref{sec:trust_filter}, reusing its already-cached reference templates. This flagged 487 of 8\,309 candidates (5.9\%) as untrustworthy, which were excluded from judging entirely, so that a summary describing the wrong entity could not poison a relevance label built on top of it.

\paragraph{Multi-judge relevance labelling.} Each remaining, trustworthy candidate was independently judged by two large language models (OpenAI's \texttt{gpt-4o-mini} and Anthropic's Claude Haiku), on a three-level scale (0~=~not relevant, 1~=~partially relevant, 2~=~highly relevant), following the LLM-as-judge approach validated at scale by \citet{thomas2024llmjudge}. Where the two judges agreed, that label was accepted directly; where they disagreed, the case was resolved by manual review.

\paragraph{Blind calibration of the judges.} Resolving disagreements by reading both judges' stated reasoning risks anchoring the human reviewer toward whichever judge's framing is read first. To check this, a separate blind sample of 20 candidates that neither judge had yet seen was labelled by the author with no access to either judge's output, then scored independently by both judges. OpenAI matched the blind label 75\% of the time and Claude 80\%, close enough that neither judge should be treated as categorically more reliable than the other on this task.

\paragraph{Scope of this pilot.} Labelling all 101 queries with two paid LLM judges was not pursued in the time available; instead, a 5-query pilot was completed first. Of 430 candidates judged, 360 (83.7\%) received unanimous agreement between the two judges. Of the remaining 70 disagreements, 59 were resolved by manual review and 11 were excluded outright for insufficient evidence (for example, unreachable domains or placeholder websites with no verifiable content), rather than being forced into a label the available information could not support. This yields \textbf{419 gold-labelled candidates} across 5 queries, distributed as 8 not relevant, 119 partially relevant, and 292 highly relevant.

\subsection{Retraining Against the New Gold Standard}
\label{subsec:refit_fusion_ranker}

The exact architecture from Section~\ref{sec:learned_fusion} (the same ten features, the same 5-fold cross-validation grouped by \texttt{query\_id}, here equivalent to leave-one-query-out since exactly five queries are covered) was retrained using the 417 of 419 gold-labelled candidates that matched an existing feature row from the original candidate pool. The binary classification target used the strict threshold \texttt{gold\_label} = 2 (highly relevant only, 291 positive vs.\ 126 negative examples); the more permissive threshold \texttt{gold\_label} $\geq$ 1 leaves only 8 negative examples across the whole pilot, too few for cross-validated coefficients to be estimated reliably.

\paragraph{Finding.} Retraining on the pooled, independently judged gold standard modestly improves ranking quality over simply reusing the original model's scores (NDCG@5: 0.915~$\to$~0.934; NDCG@10: 0.930~$\to$~0.952), confirming that the new labels carry a genuine, learnable signal rather than just noise. More importantly, the original interpretive finding from Section~\ref{sec:learned_fusion} does not replicate: \texttt{invrank\_minilm}, previously the dominant feature by a wide margin ($+5.014$, more than double the next-largest coefficient), collapses not merely toward zero but to a small \emph{negative} coefficient ($-0.909$). The most plausible explanation is that MiniLM's ranking correlated strongly with production's own circular output specifically, rather than with relevance itself, a correlation that a model trained and evaluated against that same circular output could not distinguish from a genuine relevance signal. \texttt{score\_linq} (Linq-Embed-Mistral's raw similarity score) is the new dominant feature ($+1.965$), and \texttt{invrank\_bm25} is now strongly negative ($-1.252$): being ranked highly by BM25 is associated with \emph{lower} true relevance in this sample. This corroborates, independently and statistically, the qualitative BM25 failure mode already documented in Section~\ref{subsec:bm25_qual}, where investment firms and venture capital companies are ranked highly by BM25 for the query ``software companies'' because they mention the word in passing while describing their investment portfolio, without themselves being software companies.

\paragraph{Confirmation at full scale.} The Limitations paragraph below originally flagged this as a 5-query pilot whose specific coefficients should be read cautiously. Section~\ref{subsec:active_learning}'s expanded gold standard (1{,}429 candidates across all 101 queries) makes it possible to check this directly: retraining the same architecture on the full 101-query gold standard (rightmost column of Table~\ref{tab:pooled_weights}) does not weaken the finding, if anything it sharpens it. \texttt{invrank\_minilm} remains negative ($-1.095$, was $-0.909$ at 5 queries), and \texttt{invrank\_bm25} becomes even more strongly negative ($-2.246$, was $-1.252$), now the single largest-magnitude coefficient in the model. \texttt{score\_linq} remains the dominant positive feature throughout all three columns. The BM25 vocabulary-mismatch failure mode is therefore not an artefact of the ``software companies'' pilot query specifically, it holds consistently once every one of the 101 query topics contributes labelled examples.

\paragraph{Limitations.} This result covers 5 of 101 queries and 417 candidates, a genuine pilot rather than a full replication, and the specific numerical coefficients should be read with that scale in mind. However, the magnitude of the reversal on what was previously the single dominant feature is unlikely to be an artefact of sample size alone, and is treated here as a preliminary but suggestive finding: the interpretive claims drawn from a model trained on circular pseudo-relevance labels should not be taken as descriptions of genuine relevance without independent validation. Scaling this pooled, multi-judge gold standard to the full 101-query set is a direction for future work.

% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Scaling Beyond the Gold Standard: Silver Labels via Model Distillation}
\label{sec:silver_labels}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\textit{Note: this section documents in-progress work and will be trimmed or restructured once results are finalised.}

Extending independent LLM judging to every candidate the retrieval systems in this thesis actually produce is not affordable. The original fusion ranker's own candidate pool (Section~\ref{sec:learned_fusion}) covers 173\,262 (query, candidate) pairs across all 101 queries; judging every one of those with two LLM judges, at the per-candidate cost observed in Section~\ref{subsec:pooled_gold_standard}, would cost approximately \$22 at the current pooling depth, or as much as \$28\,000 if every one of the 98\,716 corpus companies were judged against every one of the 101 queries. The latter figure is not just expensive but methodologically wasteful: the overwhelming majority of those pairs are companies that no retrieval channel ever considered plausible for that query, judging them would mean paying to confirm the obvious.

\paragraph{Approach.} Instead, the 419 gold-labelled candidates from Section~\ref{subsec:pooled_gold_standard} are used to train a final classifier (the same architecture and feature set as Section~\ref{sec:learned_fusion}, fit on all 419 labels rather than held out via cross-validation, since this model is intended for deployment rather than evaluation). This classifier is then applied to all 173\,262 existing candidate rows, which already have the required ten features computed, at no additional retrieval or labelling cost. This produces two explicitly distinct label tiers, following standard practice in weak and distant supervision: a small \textbf{gold} tier (419 candidates, LLM-judged and human-adjudicated) and a much larger \textbf{silver} tier (the remaining candidates, model-predicted rather than independently judged). The two tiers are not merged or treated as equally reliable anywhere in this pipeline.

\paragraph{Validating the silver tier.} To estimate how trustworthy the silver labels actually are, a random sample of 150 silver-labelled candidates is independently judged by two more capable models, \texttt{claude-sonnet-5} and \texttt{gpt-5.4} (an upgrade from the \texttt{claude-haiku-4-5}/\texttt{gpt-4o-mini} pair used for the original gold standard, chosen after confirming via the blind calibration check in Section~\ref{subsec:pooled_gold_standard} that the cheaper pair was not itself the limiting factor). Only candidates where both stronger judges unanimously agree are counted, disagreements are excluded from the spot-check rather than guessed at, the same principle used throughout Section~\ref{subsec:pooled_gold_standard}. This validation costs under \$1, compared to the \$22\,000+ a full re-judging pass would require.

\paragraph{Status.} The silver-labelling classifier has been trained and applied to all 173\,262 candidates. Of the 150 spot-checked silver candidates, 132 (88\%) received unanimous agreement between \texttt{gpt-5.4} and \texttt{claude-sonnet-5}; against that unanimous subset, the silver labels achieved 65.9\% accuracy (precision on the relevant class 65.3\%, recall 85.3\%). A naive baseline that always predicts the majority class on this same 132-candidate sample would score 56.8\%, so the silver labels carry a real, learnable signal, but a modest one: the classifier over-predicts relevance (34 false positives against 11 false negatives out of 132), consistent with the 75.4\% overall relevant-rate it assigns across the full silver tier.

The most likely explanation for the gap between this figure and the substantially higher NDCG improvement reported in Section~\ref{subsec:refit_fusion_ranker} is a training-distribution mismatch rather than a flaw in the distillation approach itself: only 8 of the 132 unanimously-judged spot-check candidates (6\%) fall under one of the 5 queries the underlying gold standard was built from, the remaining 124 (94\%) come from query topics the classifier never saw a single labelled example of during training. Generalising from 5 narrow query topics to the other 96 is a substantially harder test than the leave-one-query-out cross-validation in Section~\ref{subsec:refit_fusion_ranker}, which only ever held out one of those same 5 topics at a time. This points toward a concrete, affordable fix rather than abandoning the approach: expanding the gold standard to a larger, more topically diverse subset of the 101 queries (at the roughly \$22 cost estimated above for full-depth pooling) before the resulting silver labels are relied on broadly, rather than distilling from 5 queries alone.

\subsection{Expanding the Gold Standard via Active Learning}
\label{subsec:active_learning}

\textit{Note: this subsection also documents in-progress work.}

Rather than judging a random or exhaustive sample of the remaining 96 uncovered queries, the gold standard is expanded using \emph{uncertainty sampling}: the distillation classifier's own predicted probability is used to find, for every one of the 101 queries, the ten candidates it is least confident about (predicted probability closest to 0.5), and only those are sent for judging. This targets exactly the candidates most likely to teach the classifier something it does not already know, rather than spending judging budget on cases it already calls correctly. The judging prompt is enriched at the same time with company metadata that was previously collected but never shown to the judges: \texttt{organization\_type}, \texttt{organization\_size}, \texttt{state}, \texttt{district}, \texttt{municipality}, and \texttt{nace\_code}, alongside the original name, country, and summary fields, giving the LLM judges more grounding for cases a summary alone leaves ambiguous. This active-learning approach is well established in the machine learning literature \citep{settles2009active}, and combining LLM-based and human-provided relevance judgments to build a test collection under a limited labelling budget mirrors the approach of \citet{takehi2024llmassisted}, who propose a similar strategy for balancing manual and LLM annotation effort in IR test collection construction.

\paragraph{Result.} This active-learning pass covers all 101 queries (versus the original 5) and adds 1{,}010 newly judged candidates, 852 (84.4\%) agreed unanimously between the two judges, 158 needed human tie-breaking, a disagreement rate close to the original pilot's 16\% once an initial data-merging bug that had corrupted roughly 40\% of the first labelling pass with a missing query field was found and fixed. Combined with the original 419, the expanded gold standard now contains \textbf{1{,}429 candidates spanning all 101 queries} (label distribution: 63 not relevant, 381 partially relevant, 985 highly relevant), a nearly 3.5$\times$ increase in size and full query coverage instead of a 5\% sample of it.

\paragraph{Result.} The distillation classifier was retrained on the full 1{,}427 gold-labelled candidates matched to features (spanning all 101 queries) and re-applied to all 173\,262 candidates. A fresh 150-candidate spot-check was drawn (a different random sample than the first, so as not to reuse previously-checked candidates) and judged with the same stronger pair, \texttt{gpt-5.4} and \texttt{claude-sonnet-5}. Of these, 140 (93.3\%) received unanimous agreement, slightly higher than the first spot-check's 88\%. Against that unanimous subset, silver-label accuracy rose from \textbf{65.9\% to 80.7\%}. The naive majority-class baseline also shifted with the new sample (56.8\% $\to$ 64.3\%), so the more informative comparison is the \emph{margin} over that baseline: it nearly doubled, from 9.1 percentage points to 16.4. The earlier bias toward over-predicting relevance is substantially reduced: false positives fell from 34/132 (25.8\%) to 11/140 (7.9\%), precision on the relevant class rose from 65.3\% to 87.1\%, and recall held roughly steady (85.3\% $\to$ 82.2\%), for an overall F1 improvement from 0.740 to 0.846. Consistent with a less relevance-biased model, the predicted-relevant rate across the full silver tier dropped from 75.4\% to 61.2\%. Finally, and most directly confirming the original diagnosis: 140 of 140 (100\%) of the unanimously-judged spot-check candidates this time fell under a query the classifier had been trained on, compared to only 8 of 132 (6\%) previously. The out-of-distribution generalisation gap identified in the first spot-check was not a flaw in the distillation approach itself, it was a direct consequence of training on 5 of 101 query topics, and expanding query coverage resolved it.

\paragraph{A further possible extension (not yet implemented).} A complementary, cheaper way to grow labelled data further, without spending additional judging budget at all, would be to propagate labels from already-judged candidates to their nearest neighbours in embedding space: since every candidate already has multiple similarity scores computed against its query (Section~\ref{sec:learned_fusion}), an unjudged candidate that is highly similar to a known highly-relevant gold-labelled company for the same query could plausibly inherit a provisional label, following the classic label propagation approach of \citet{zhu2002labelpropagation}. This is distinct from the distillation classifier already in use here, which learns a general relevance function from retrieval features, rather than propagating labels from specific labelled neighbours, and the two could in principle be combined or compared. This is left as a direction for future work rather than implemented in this thesis.

\subsection{Deepening a Headline Query Subset via Round-Based Active Learning}
\label{subsec:headline_deepening}

The shallow, roughly uniform depth left by Section~\ref{subsec:active_learning}'s expansion (10-14 gold-labelled candidates per query) fixes out-of-distribution generalisation but is too thin to report reliable per-query Recall@$k$/NDCG@$k$ as an evaluation benchmark, deeper judged pools reduce the variance of such metrics (Section~\ref{sec:scaling_corpus} discusses this further). Rather than deepening all 101 queries, a set of 14 queries was selected for methodological diversity beyond simple topic variety: three testing geography not covered by the original pilot, one directly probing the BM25 vocabulary-mismatch failure mode (``venture capital firms''), four forming two adjacent-category disambiguation pairs (pharmaceutical/biotech, investment/wealth management), two acronym-heavy queries, two niche or emerging industries, and two further sector-diversity queries.

Unlike the one-shot batch in Section~\ref{subsec:active_learning}, this used genuinely iterative active learning: label a round, retrain the classifier on everything labelled so far, re-rank the \emph{remaining} candidates with the \emph{updated} classifier, and repeat, since uncertainty estimates go stale as soon as new labels are added. A small fixed validation set (100 candidates, judged once with the stronger judge pair and reused for free across every round) tracked whether each additional round was still worth its cost.

\paragraph{Refreshing the silver tier at no additional cost.} The combined 2{,}269-candidate gold standard (1{,}429 from Section~\ref{subsec:active_learning} plus 840 from this section) was used to retrain the distillation classifier from Section~\ref{sec:silver_labels} once more, and applied to the existing candidate pool to produce up to 1{,}000 silver-labelled candidates per query, capped rather than kept at the full pool size, and with every gold-verified candidate guaranteed a place regardless of its predicted probability rank. This required no new retrieval, encoding, or judging: every one of the 101 queries already has at least 1{,}022 candidates with retrieval features computed (median 1{,}661), so the only new work is classifier inference. All 101 queries now have exactly 1{,}000 candidates each (101{,}000 rows total, 2{,}267 of them gold-verified), the deepest per-query coverage produced so far in this thesis, though still silver-tier and bounded by the classifier's demonstrated accuracy (Section~\ref{subsec:headline_deepening}) rather than independently verified.

\subsection{Is the Classifier's Confidence Honest? A Calibration Check}
\label{subsec:calibration_check}

\subsection{Honest Synthesis: Is the Gold/Silver Standard Better Than GOI's Own Output?}
\label{subsec:honest_synthesis}

Pulling together every check in Sections~\ref{sec:silver_labels}--\ref{subsec:calibration_check}, the answer is not a single yes or no, it depends on which specific claim is being made, and the evidence does not point the same way for each:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Is the labelling methodology more defensible than trusting production's own output as ground truth?} Yes. Production's ranking cannot validate itself; it is circular by construction (Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction}). This thesis's gold standard uses independent multi-judge LLM labelling, blind calibration (Section~\ref{subsec:pooled_gold_standard}), and human tie-breaking, none of which production's own ranking has any equivalent of.
\item \textbf{Does this thesis's methodology find relevant companies production's own system misses entirely?} Yes, demonstrated: 27.3\% of gold-verified highly relevant companies are entirely absent from production's top-1{,}000 (Section~\ref{subsec:production_divergence}, Check 3).
\item \textbf{Does this thesis's ranking algorithm outperform production's ranking at retrieving relevant companies, head-to-head?} Not yet shown, and the one direct test conducted (Section~\ref{subsec:goi_vs_ours_ranking}) found the opposite, likely attributable to residual candidate-pool bias rather than a genuine quality reversal, but this has not been independently confirmed.
\item \textbf{Is the classifier itself highly accurate and well-calibrated?} Only moderately. Brier score 0.220, with a real, systematic calibration gap in the low-to-middle confidence range (Section~\ref{subsec:calibration_check}).
\end{itemize}

The defensible claim this thesis supports is therefore narrower than ``the new labels are better'': \emph{the independent gold standard is methodologically sounder for evaluation purposes and demonstrates concrete, measurable blind spots in production's own ranking, but this thesis has not yet shown that its own ranking algorithm retrieves relevant companies more effectively than production does.} Confirming or refuting that stronger claim requires evaluating against a gold-verified relevant set drawn from candidates production had no part in surfacing, which motivates the random-sample-based evaluation described next.

\paragraph{Limitation: precision is a lower bound, not an exact figure.} Every precision@$k$ number reported in this thesis is computed against the pooled, actively-sampled gold standard, not an exhaustive judgment of every retrieved candidate. A retrieved company only counts as a true positive if it happens to have already been gold-labelled; a genuinely relevant company that was simply never pooled or judged contributes neither a true positive nor a false positive; it is silently invisible to the metric. Recall@$k$ is unaffected by this, since it is computed only over the set of candidates that do have a gold label. Precision@$k$, however, is systematically biased downward by an unknown amount, since some fraction of a method's retrieved-but-unlabelled candidates are almost certainly relevant and simply were never reviewed. This is a known consequence of evaluating against pooled rather than exhaustive judgments \citep{baryossef2008random, lu2016effect}, not a flaw specific to this thesis's pipeline, but it means precision figures throughout should be read as conservative lower bounds rather than exact values.

\paragraph{Future work: tightening this bound with more exhaustive LLM ranking.} A more exhaustive per-query relevance pass, an LLM directly ranking or scoring every candidate in an already-pooled, multi-source candidate set (rather than the smaller, actively-sampled subset actually judged in this thesis), would shrink the gap between reported and true precision by leaving fewer retrieved candidates unjudged. This must still draw its candidates from a pooled, multi-source set rather than a single retrieval method's own output, otherwise the same single-source circularity this thesis's gold standard was built to avoid (Section~\ref{subsec:pooled_gold_standard}) reappears one layer downstream, with the LLM only ever able to rank whatever one method chose to surface. Scaling this idea further, toward Istari's full company database rather than the $\sim$98\,716-\,to-397\,025-company samples used throughout this thesis, is a natural direction: the same pool-then-judge structure would need to repeat at that scale, with the resulting broader, automated relevance judgments used both as a larger gold standard and as the basis for the production top-1{,}000 retrieval this thesis's methods ultimately aim to support. Neither extension is implemented here; both are left as concrete next steps.

\subsection{Hyperparameter Tuning the Fusion Ranker}
\label{subsec:hyperparameter_tuning}

Every fusion ranker result reported so far, the original model (Section~\ref{sec:learned_fusion}), the pooled-gold-standard retraining (Section~\ref{subsec:refit_fusion_ranker}), and the cross-validated ranking comparison against production (Section~\ref{subsec:goi_vs_ours_ranking}), uses the same hand-picked \texttt{HistGradientBoostingClassifier} configuration (\texttt{max\_iter=150}, \texttt{max\_depth=4}, \texttt{class\_weight="balanced"}), chosen once early on and never systematically tuned against the gold standard. This section addresses that gap with a randomized hyperparameter search.

\paragraph{Method.} The search space covers \texttt{max\_depth} (2 to 8, or unlimited), \texttt{max\_iter} (50 to 300), \texttt{learning\_rate} (0.03 to 0.3), \texttt{min\_samples\_leaf} (5 to 30), and \texttt{l2\_regularization} (0.0 to 2.0); \texttt{class\_weight="balanced"} is kept fixed throughout, since it is an established, justified design choice rather than a model-capacity hyperparameter. 40 combinations are sampled at random (fixed seed for reproducibility).

\paragraph{Avoiding leakage in model selection.} Selecting the best combination using the same cross-validated metric later reported as the final result would bias that result upward (the ``winner's curse'' in model selection): a combination is not a fair, unbiased estimate of unseen-data performance if it was chosen \emph{because} it scored best on exactly the data used to report it. To avoid this, the search itself is restricted to the 82 queries without deep gold coverage, evaluated with \texttt{LeaveOneGroupOut} cross-validation grouped by \texttt{query\_id} within that 82-query pool only, scored by mean Recall@1{,}000 and NDCG@1{,}000. The 19 deep-coverage queries (5-query pilot plus 14 headline queries) are never touched during the search. Once the best combination is fixed, it is evaluated fresh with a full \texttt{LeaveOneGroupOut} pass over all 101 queries, the same protocol used for the untuned baseline (Section~\ref{subsec:goi_vs_ours_ranking}). The 19-deep-query subset of that final evaluation is a genuinely leakage-free comparison against the untuned baseline, since those queries never influenced which hyperparameters were chosen; the all-101-query subset is reported alongside it for comparability with earlier sections, but should be read as directional rather than strictly unbiased, since the other 82 queries did inform the search.

\paragraph{Results.} The best combination found by the search (selected on the 82 tuning-only queries) was \texttt{max\_depth=3}, \texttt{max\_iter=50}, \texttt{learning\_rate=0.03}, \texttt{min\_samples\_leaf=10}, \texttt{l2\_regularization=0.5}, notably shallower and using far fewer boosting iterations than the original hand-picked configuration (\texttt{max\_depth=4}, \texttt{max\_iter=150}).

\subsection{Fine-Tuning the Reranker}
\label{subsec:reranker_finetuning}

Every use of \texttt{BAAI/bge-reranker-v2-m3} throughout this thesis, in the hybrid reranking pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:hybrid_fullpool}) and as one of the ten fusion-ranker features, treats it as a frozen, pretrained component, never fit to this thesis's own queries or labels. This section fine-tunes it directly on the gold-labelled (query, candidate) pairs and checks whether that improves ranking quality, reusing the same 82-tuning/19-held-out query split as Section~\ref{subsec:hyperparameter_tuning} rather than introducing a new one, so the two experiments stay directly comparable and the held-out queries remain untouched by either.

\paragraph{Method.} The reranker is fine-tuned as a regression problem: for each gold-labelled (query, candidate-summary) pair from the 82 tuning-only queries ($\sim$1{,}000 examples), the target score is \texttt{gold\_label}/2 (mapping 0/1/2 to 0.0/0.5/1.0, matching the reranker's own continuous score output), trained with MSE loss, AdamW, learning rate $2\times10^{-5}$, batch size 32, for 4 epochs. This is a small dataset for a $\sim$568M-parameter model, a real overfitting risk flagged explicitly rather than glossed over. Both the original pretrained reranker and the fine-tuned version are then evaluated on the same task the reranker actually performs in this pipeline: reranking each held-out query's full candidate pool from the union-pool feature set (Section~\ref{sec:learned_fusion}, $\sim$1{,}000-2{,}900 candidates per query) and scoring the result by Recall@$k$/NDCG@$k$ against the gold-verified relevant set, restricted to the 19 deep-coverage queries so the comparison is leakage-free by the same logic as Section~\ref{subsec:hyperparameter_tuning}.

\paragraph{Status.} This experiment has been implemented but not yet run; results, including whether fine-tuning improves on the pretrained reranker and any precision/recall trade-off analogous to the one found in Section~\ref{subsec:hyperparameter_tuning}, will be reported here once available.

\section{Scaling to a Larger Corpus: a Random Sample and Nested Corpus Tiers}
\label{sec:scaling_corpus}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\textit{Note: this section documents in-progress work.}

The 98\,716-company corpus used throughout this thesis is not just query-gatekept (Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction}), it is also small relative to Istari's real production dataset. The thesis title's ``at scale'' claim needs a genuine scalability test, does the retrieval pipeline's quality and latency hold up as the pool of companies to search grows substantially larger, not just a claim resting on the existing corpus size.

\paragraph{A fully random sample.} A collaborator at Istari (Kerem Cerit) provided a fully random, query-independent sample of 300\,000 companies drawn from the underlying database, deliberately not filtered or weighted toward any of the 101 queries used elsewhere in this thesis. Randomness here matters methodologically: using this sample to check whether a retrieval system's index or corpus omits genuinely relevant material, rather than to build more query-specific training data, follows the same logic as random-sampling approaches used to estimate search engine index coverage \citep{baryossef2008random}, applied here to check whether the retrieval pipeline still finds known-relevant companies once far more distractor companies are added, rather than to estimate absolute index coverage as in that work.

\paragraph{Data quality.} The sample was profiled and lightly cleaned (data profiling script, not included as a numbered notebook section here for brevity). Of note: only 0.56\% (1\,691 companies) overlapped with the existing 98\,716-company corpus, confirming the sample is genuinely independent rather than a re-draw of existing data. No duplicate domains, no empty summaries, and no literal-string placeholder values were found. State/district/municipality fields were missing 15.7\%/60.4\%/75.4\% of the time respectively, expected sparsity given inconsistent administrative-division reporting across countries, not a data quality defect. A small fraction of rows (1.03\%) shared an exact-duplicate summary with another row, flagged rather than dropped, since a shared summary does not necessarily indicate an invalid company record.

\paragraph{Nested corpus tiers.} Rather than four independently drawn samples of increasing size, the scaling corpus is built as four \emph{nested} tiers: 100\,000, 200\,000, 300\,000, and $\sim$397\,000 (all available) companies, each strictly containing the previous tier plus additional randomly drawn companies from one fixed shuffle. The existing 98\,716-company corpus, which holds all gold-labelled data used for evaluation, is included in every tier without exception, so that a change in a retrieval metric between tiers can be attributed to the added distractor companies, not to gold-labelled companies being dropped from the search pool. This nested design keeps the four tiers directly comparable to one another, following the same logic that motivates careful control of pooling depth in retrieval evaluation more broadly \citep{lu2016effect}.

\paragraph{Encoding.} Every company in the combined pool ($\sim$397\,025 companies) is encoded exactly once with each embedding model, rather than re-encoded separately per tier: an embedding is a fixed, corpus-independent property of a single company's text, so encoding once and subsetting afterward is both correct and avoids redundant computation. BM25 is a deliberate exception, being a corpus-level statistical model rather than a per-document encoding (a term's weight depends on its frequency across the whole indexed collection), its index must be rebuilt separately for each of the four tiers once the scaling evaluation itself is run. The pretrained reranker (\texttt{bge-reranker-v2-m3}) is not part of this encoding step at all, it scores (query, candidate) pairs at retrieval time only, applied to whichever candidates survive first-stage retrieval, regardless of corpus size.

\paragraph{Scope: beyond the three fusion-ranker features.} The scaled encoding was not limited to the three models already inside the trained fusion ranker (MiniLM, GTE-large, Linq-Embed-Mistral). Every embedding model evaluated as a baseline earlier in this thesis (BGE-large, BGE-M3, Nomic, E5-Mistral, SFR-Mistral, SimLM) and ColBERTv2 is also being encoded at the new scale. The motivation is the same circularity concern raised in Section~\ref{subsec:refit_fusion_ranker}: the original baseline comparison chapter, including the finding that BGE underperforms MiniLM, was evaluated entirely against the circular \texttt{goi\_search\_results.json} ground truth, before the independent, pooled gold standard existed, and has never been rechecked against it, unlike the fusion ranker's own feature-importance finding, which was rechecked and found to partially reverse (Section~\ref{subsec:refit_fusion_ranker}). Re-scoring every baseline model's already-computed top-1000 rankings against the current non-circular, 1{,}429-candidate gold standard, at zero additional API or GPU cost, is planned as a follow-up to check whether the reported model ranking (MiniLM best, BGE and BGE-M3 below it) survives independent verification the same way the fusion ranker's finding was checked, consistent with the general principle that pooling depth and evaluation-set independence, not just raw retrieval scores, determine how trustworthy a reported ranking of methods actually is \citep{baryossef2008random}.

ColBERT is architecturally distinct from every other model here: it stores one embedding per token rather than one vector per company (`doc\_embs.pt`, shape $[N, 192, 128]$, roughly 19.5\,GB at this scale versus 4.85\,GB for the original 98,716-company run), so it cannot simply be subset into corpus-size tiers the same way a single dense vector per company can; it is included nonetheless, for completeness against every method previously evaluated.

\paragraph{Status.} The combined pool and nested tier membership have been constructed, and encoding is essentially complete: 9 of the 10 models (MiniLM, GTE-large, BGE-large, Nomic, Linq-Embed-Mistral, E5-Mistral, SFR-Mistral, SimLM, ColBERTv2) have produced a full embedding file covering all $\sim$397\,025 companies; BGE-M3 is the last one still running. The original 98\,716-company Linq-Embed-Mistral run took approximately 34 minutes of GPU time, spread across several resubmitted jobs due to this cluster's per-job wall-time limit and lengthy model-loading time, at roughly 4$\times$ the scale, encoding times for the large AutoModel-based encoders turned out shorter than that estimate once a Hugging Face Hub network-verification hang was diagnosed and fixed (Section~\ref{sec:scaling_corpus}'s encoding pipeline), typically well under 30 minutes of pure encoding once loading succeeds.

Two evaluation notebooks have been built to answer question (1), does Recall@$k$/NDCG@$k$ and query latency hold up as the corpus grows from roughly 100K to 400K companies, restricted to the 19 queries with the deepest and most reliable gold-standard coverage (the 5-query pilot plus the 14 headline queries from Section~\ref{subsec:active_learning}), so the resulting metrics are not read from an unreliably shallow label set. A per-tier BM25 index notebook builds a separate index for each of the four nested tiers (tokenizing the corpus once and reusing the tokenization across tiers, since only the IDF statistics change with tier size, not the tokens themselves). A scaling-evaluation notebook then covers BM25 alongside the three embedding models already inside the trained fusion ranker (MiniLM, GTE-large, Linq-Embed-Mistral), computing Recall@$k$/NDCG@$k$ against the gold standard and per-query retrieval latency at every tier, for every method. Every gold-relevant candidate for the 19 deep queries is present in all four tiers by construction, confirmed directly (906 of 906 relevant candidates found in even the smallest, 100K, tier), so any change in these metrics across tiers is attributable to the growing pool of distractor companies rather than relevant companies dropping out of the search pool. Both notebooks are built and ready to run once the last encoder finishes; results will be reported here once available. Question (2), whether the original baseline-model ranking survives re-evaluation against the non-circular gold standard, remains a separate follow-up not yet started.
