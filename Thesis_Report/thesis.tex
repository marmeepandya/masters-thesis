\chapter{Abstract}

A company that is a perfect match for a search query can still be invisible to a keyword-based search system, simply because its own description never repeats the query's exact wording. This vocabulary mismatch problem sits at the centre of a concrete question for ISTARI.AI's company search product: can embedding-based semantic retrieval, paired with approximate nearest-neighbour search, generate accurate candidate sets efficiently at scale, and does it actually outperform the keyword-driven production system currently in use? Comparing more than ten dense embedding models, a late-interaction architecture, and several hybrid pipelines over a corpus of 98,716 companies, a learned fusion ranker trained directly on this task's own queries reaches the strongest result under the standard protocol, Recall@1,000~$=$~0.823.

That number turns out to rest on shakier ground than it first appears. No human relevance judgements exist for this task, so the only available yardstick is production's own output, a circular standard that can never show a system beats production if production itself defines what counts as correct. This thesis measures exactly how much that circularity was hiding. Retrained against an independently built, multi-judge silver standard, the fusion ranker's own interpretive model reverses one of its most confident conclusions, and production's own results turn out to miss 27.3\% of independently verified relevant companies outright. Tested on a pool of companies production had never even ranked, the same pipeline recovers 88.5\% of them, the strongest evidence yet that this coverage gain is genuine rather than an artefact of how the evaluation was built.

At the largest tested scale, 397,025 companies, roughly 2\% of Istari's full database, approximate nearest-neighbour search matches exact search's retrieval quality within 2.5\% while running up to 78 times faster, a gap confirmed by statistical testing rather than asserted from raw numbers alone, establishing a favourable trend at the scale actually tested rather than a claim about full production scale. Together, these results make a concrete case that semantic retrieval can outperform a keyword-based production system on both accuracy and speed, and that trusting a production system's own output as ground truth, a common shortcut when no human-labelled data exists, deserves far more scrutiny than it typically receives.

\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

A company that is a strong match for a search query can still be invisible to a keyword-based search system, simply because its own description uses different words than the query does. A search for a \textit{``renewable energy consultancy''}, for example, will not surface a company that describes itself as a \textit{``sustainability advisory service''}, even though the company may be exactly the kind of business the query is looking for. This is a well-known limitation of classical keyword-based retrieval. Sales teams targeting prospects, investors screening deals, and analysts mapping a market or a supply chain all depend on locating the right handful of relevant organisations out of a much larger pool, often millions of candidates. Providers such as ISTARI.AI build company intelligence platforms specifically to support this need, maintaining datasets containing tens of millions of organisation profiles, covering everything from small local businesses to large multinational enterprises. Existing workflows at these platforms, however, typically rely on exactly the manually configured keyword filters that are vulnerable to the mismatch problem described above.

At the same time, any system intended to operate on a company database of this size must satisfy strict constraints on efficiency and scalability. Retrieval has to remain accurate over a dataset that is high-cardinality in a specific sense. It spans tens of millions of distinct organisation profiles, any one of which could be the correct answer to a given query, rather than a small, fixed set of categories a classifier could simply learn to distinguish. It also has to return results quickly enough to support an interactive search experience, not just a slow, offline batch process.

This thesis is conducted in collaboration with ISTARI.AI (Chapter~\ref{ch:istariAndData} introduces the company and its data), which grounds the thesis in a real operational setting.

\paragraph{Research context and gap.} Dense, embedding-based retrieval and approximate nearest-neighbour search are well established individually: bi-encoder architectures, late-interaction models, and hybrid fusion methods have all been shown to outperform keyword matching on standard information retrieval benchmarks \citep{karpukhin2020dense} (Chapter~\ref{ch:related_literature} reviews this literature in full). What is missing is evidence for how these methods behave on large-scale company profiles specifically, an applied setting closer to entity-level retrieval and company-embedding work \citep{gerling2023company} than to general-purpose passage benchmarks, where records combine heterogeneous structured fields with AI-generated free text. This is the first gap this thesis addresses: applying established retrieval methods to a domain with genuinely different data characteristics.

\paragraph{Evaluation gap.} A second, deeper gap concerns evaluation itself. Existing retrieval research is predominantly validated against benchmarks with human relevance judgements. For Istari's company-search task, no independently constructed relevance judgements were available for the full corpus and query set at the start of this project, since Istari's 20-million-company database has never been exhaustively labelled by anyone. In their absence, a natural fallback is to treat production's own retrieved companies as a proxy for relevance, a practical benchmark when human judgements are unavailable~\citep{voorhees2002philosophy, thakur2021beir}. Such a proxy is circular by construction, however: it can support operational comparisons between systems, but it cannot establish whether a competing system retrieves relevant companies that production itself misses. This thesis treats that circularity as part of the research problem rather than a limitation to note in passing, and constructs an independent relevance standard, built incrementally rather than all at once (Section~\ref{sec:silver_labels}), to test it directly.

\paragraph{Problem statement.} Put directly, this thesis addresses one specific, technical problem: it is not known whether embedding-based semantic retrieval can outperform Istari's existing keyword-based production system, at production-relevant scale and query latency, and it is not known whether any such comparison, as currently measured, is valid at all.

\paragraph{Research questions.} This problem translates into three research questions, addressed in turn across Chapters~\ref{ch:methodology} and~\ref{ch:results}:

\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textbf{RQ1}] Can embedding-based semantic retrieval, combined with approximate nearest-neighbour search, generate accurate top-$k$ candidate company sets from a large-scale dataset while keeping query latency low, and how does its retrieval quality compare to Istari's keyword-based production system?
\item[\textbf{RQ2}] Does combining multiple, complementary retrieval signals, culminating in a model trained directly on this task's own queries, retrieve more relevant companies than any single retrieval method alone?
\item[\textbf{RQ3}] Is the production-derived evaluation protocol used to answer RQ1 and RQ2 itself trustworthy, and do the retrieval gains observed under it still hold once tested against an independently constructed, non-circular relevance standard?
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Contributions.} This thesis makes five main contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
\item A controlled, common-protocol comparison of retrieval approaches for company search, covering lexical retrieval, dense bi-encoder retrieval across more than ten models spanning different sizes and training objectives, late-interaction retrieval, and retrieval-specific pretraining, all evaluated on the same corpus, query set, and metrics.
\item A hybrid and learned retrieval framework that combines complementary retrieval signals, culminating in a fusion ranker trained on this task's own queries that outperforms every pipeline built from frozen, pretrained components under the protocol it is trained and evaluated against, together with an interpretable account of which retrieval signals drive relevance.
\item An analysis of the circularity inherent in production-derived pseudo-relevance, together with a production-independent silver standard, built from external LLM judgements and human adjudication, testing whether the production-based evaluation's conclusions, including the fusion ranker's own findings, still hold once that circularity is removed.
\item An analysis of the accuracy-efficiency trade-off of approximate nearest-neighbour search across corpus sizes of up to 397,025 companies, with a latency analysis separating local computation from network overhead for API-based methods.
\item A quantified data-quality issue in which some query topics, most severely ``web hosting companies'' (85.4\% of the companies judged relevant to that query), have AI-generated summaries that describe a website hosting or platform provider rather than the company itself.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Scope.} These contributions come with explicit boundaries. ``At scale'' throughout this thesis means the largest corpus directly evaluated. This corpus reaches nested tiers of up to 397,025 companies (Section~\ref{sec:scaling_corpus}), roughly 2\% of Istari's full 20-million-company database. It does not mean an end-to-end evaluation at full production scale. The experiments establish a scalability trend up to the tested size, and Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} discusses what would be needed to extend it further. This thesis evaluates retrieval quality and computational efficiency under the query set and corpus described in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}. It does not evaluate the end-user search experience, run a live production A/B test, or build and deploy a production system. Every result reported here is measured offline against the datasets and pipelines described in that chapter.

\paragraph{Thesis outline.} The remainder of this thesis is organised as follows:

\begin{itemize}
\item Chapter~\ref{ch:istariAndData} introduces Istari's Global Organisation Index, the dataset underlying this thesis.
\item Chapter~\ref{ch:related_literature} reviews the relevant retrieval and evaluation literature.
\item Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} describes the experimental setup and the design of every baseline and experiment.
\item Chapter~\ref{ch:results} reports the results, findings, and interpretation for each of those experiments.
\item Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} summarises the main findings and outlines directions for future work.
\end{itemize}

\section{About ISTARI.AI and the Global Organisation Index}

This thesis is conducted in collaboration with ISTARI.AI\protect\footnote{\url{https://docs.istari.ai/goi/goi_start_page/}}, a data intelligence company specialising in firmographic data at scale. Istari's core product is a curated, organisation-level dataset covering organisations worldwide, enabling use cases such as market analysis, company search, and lead generation. The central research challenge addressed in this thesis is improving the quality of semantic company retrieval, which arises directly from Istari's operational needs.

The primary dataset used in this thesis is Istari's \textbf{Global Organisation Index} (GOI), a curated collection of approximately 20 million active organisations spanning 232 countries and territories. The GOI serves as the canonical source of firmographic data within Istari's data infrastructure, covering organisation identity, location, industry classification, organisation size, and other firmographic attributes.

\section{Data Pipeline and Methodology}

The GOI is constructed through a multi-stage validation pipeline that deliberately prioritises data quality over volume. The pipeline proceeds as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Registry Collection.} National registers are systematically queried worldwide, including commercial registers, company registers, and association registers. These primary sources are enriched with additional open data sources, yielding an initial pool of approximately 400 million organisations.

\item \textbf{Domain Attribution.} Each organisation is assessed for attribution to an identifiable web domain. Approximately 10\% of the initial pool, around 40 million organisations can be matched to a domain. The remainder are excluded as they are either no longer active, were never truly operational (e.g., pure holding structures), or lack a web presence.

\item \textbf{Activity Verification.} The 40 million domain-attributed organisations are further filtered by verifying that their domains are still actively operated. This final step reduces the dataset to approximately 20 million organisations that are demonstrably active.
\end{enumerate}

According to Istari, this pipeline results in a GOI in which every record is both web domain-verified and confirmed active. Istari positions this as a differentiator from traditional firmographic databases that typically retain many inactive or dormant records.

\subsection{Evaluation Dataset}

The full 20-million-company GOI corpus is not directly used for retrieval in this thesis. Instead, Istari provided an evaluation dataset consisting of 101 real-world search queries along with each query's top-1\,000 production results, yielding 101\,000 (query, company) pairs. The same company can legitimately appear in more than one query's top-1\,000 results, so this thesis deduplicates the 101\,000 pairs by \texttt{domain}, keeping a single record per company; after this deduplication, the evaluation corpus covers \textbf{98\,716 unique companies}. All retrieval experiments described in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} are conducted over this corpus. Consequently, the 98\,716-company corpus should not be interpreted as a random sample of the full GOI; it is derived from the companies production itself returned for the 101 queries. The exact pseudo-relevance threshold used to define ``relevant'' companies for evaluation (and how it changes across experiments) is described in Sections~\ref{subsec:eval_protocol} and~\ref{sec:protocol_note}.

Testing retrieval quality at the full 20-million-company scale was not pursued in the current phase of this thesis, since doing so would require a substantially larger data export from Istari than the query-level top-1\,000 results used here; this is discussed as an open question in Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}.

\section{Sparse Retrieval}
\label{sec:lit_sparse_dense}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Text retrieval systems fall into two broad families. \textbf{Sparse} retrieval represents queries and documents as high-dimensional vectors of term weights and scores matches by term overlap. A widely used sparse retrieval method is BM25 \citep{robertson2009probabilistic}, a probabilistic scoring function that weighs each query term by its frequency in a document and its rarity across the whole collection. BM25 is fast, requires no training, and remains a strong baseline decades after its introduction. However, it cannot reliably match queries and documents that describe the same concept using different words, a limitation known as the vocabulary mismatch problem and directly observed for this thesis's task in Section~\ref{subsec:bm25_qual}.

% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Dense Retrieval}
\label{sec:lit_dense}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\textbf{Dense} retrieval instead represents queries and documents as fixed-size vectors learned by a neural encoder, and scores matches by vector similarity rather than literal term overlap. Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR) \citep{karpukhin2020dense} popularised the bi-encoder architecture for open-domain retrieval, using separate encoders for queries and documents (or, in this thesis, companies). The two encoders are trained so that relevant query-document pairs are close in vector space, while each side is encoded independently. This allows documents to be embedded once, offline, and their representations reused across future queries. Sentence-BERT \citep{reimers2019sentence} showed that the same bi-encoder idea, applied to general-purpose sentence embeddings rather than a retrieval-specific training objective, produces embeddings useful for semantic similarity and retrieval tasks broadly, and underlies the general-purpose sentence-transformer models (including MiniLM, Section~\ref{subsec:minilm}) used throughout this thesis.

More recent embedding models, such as the BGE family \citep{xiao2024cpack,chen2024bge}, combine this bi-encoder architecture with large-scale contrastive pretraining and hard-negative mining, and have become strong general-purpose dense retrieval models, forming the basis of several baselines evaluated in Sections~\ref{subsec:bge} and~\ref{sec:extended_baselines}.

Two distinct further directions in embedding model development are also represented among this thesis's extended baselines (Section~\ref{sec:extended_baselines}): GTE \citep{li2023towardsgte} and Nomic Embed \citep{nussbaum2024nomic} show that a comparatively small (100--300M-parameter) encoder, trained with careful multi-stage contrastive learning over diverse data, can match or exceed much larger models. E5-Mistral \citep{wang2024improving}, SFR-Embedding-Mistral \citep{meng2024sfrembedding}, and Linq-Embed-Mistral \citep{linq2024technical} instead take the opposite approach, adapting an already-large 7-billion-parameter instruction-tuned language model (Mistral-7B) into an embedding model via further contrastive fine-tuning. This split between ``small and carefully trained'' and ``large and repurposed'' is directly tested for this thesis's own task in Section~\ref{sec:extended_baselines_result}, where it does not resolve in the direction model scale alone would predict.

Public leaderboards such as the Massive Text Embedding Benchmark (MTEB) \citep{muennighoff2023mteb} rank models like these against one another across many tasks, but its results, like all such leaderboards, reflect performance averaged over its constituent benchmark datasets, not any particular downstream domain. The extent to which a strong MTEB ranking transfers to company-profile retrieval specifically is itself part of what Section~\ref{sec:extended_baselines_result} investigates.

\section{Approximate Nearest Neighbour Search}
\label{sec:lit_ann}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Dense retrieval requires finding the nearest vectors to a query vector among the full document collection. Exact search costs time linear in the number of documents, which becomes impractical at the scale of millions of documents relevant to this thesis's target deployment (Section~\ref{ch:istariAndData}). Approximate nearest neighbour (ANN) search instead searches only a carefully chosen subset of the collection, avoiding a full linear scan. The central trade-off is between recall, how often the true nearest neighbours are actually found, and query latency, and unlike exact search this trade-off is tunable. It depends on the index structure and its search-time parameters, not on a single fixed accuracy cost paid by approximating at all. FAISS \citep{johnson2017billion} provides GPU-accelerated implementations of both exact and approximate search, including inverted-file indexes (IVF), which cluster the embedding space in advance and, at query time, search only the clusters nearest the query rather than every vector, reducing candidate comparisons at the cost of possibly missing a true neighbour that fell into a cluster the query never visits. Hierarchical Navigable Small World graphs (HNSW) \citep{malkov2020efficient} take a different approach. They build a multi-layer proximity graph over the document vectors at index-construction time, then greedily traverse it at query time, so the accuracy-latency trade-off is governed by how deep and wide that traversal is allowed to go rather than by how many clusters are searched. Both approaches also carry a memory and index-build cost that a linear scan does not, IVF's cluster centroids and HNSW's proximity graph must be built once, in advance, and held in memory alongside the vectors themselves, a cost this thesis's own scaling experiment tracks directly rather than assumes away. Both IVF and HNSW are evaluated directly in this thesis's ANN scaling experiment (Section~\ref{sec:ann_scaling}), which tests whether their recall-latency trade-off remains favourable as corpus size grows toward simulated production scale.

\section{Late Interaction and Retrieval-Specific Pretraining}
\label{sec:lit_late_interaction}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

A bi-encoder represents an entire document with a single fixed-size vector, which can discard fine-grained information relevant to a specific query. ColBERT \citep{khattab2020colbert} addresses this with \emph{late interaction}. Queries and documents are each represented as sets of per-token vectors, and relevance is scored by summing, for each query token, the maximum similarity to any document token (MaxSim). This avoids requiring a single vector to summarise the whole document in advance.

This adds compute and storage cost relative to a single-vector bi-encoder, since retrieval now requires comparing token sets rather than single vectors. ColBERTv2 \citep{santhanam2022colbertv2} introduces residual compression of the per-token vectors, substantially reducing this cost, and the subsequent  Performance-optimized Late Interaction Driver (PLAID) engine \citep{santhanam2022plaid} builds on that compressed representation to make late-interaction search efficient at scale. This thesis instead implements MaxSim scoring directly (Section~\ref{sec:colbert}), trading PLAID's efficiency for an exact, dependency-light implementation suited to the scale of the evaluation corpus used here.

A separate line of work improves dense retrieval not through architecture but through pretraining. SimLM \citep{wang2022simlm} pretrains a bi-encoder with a representation-bottleneck objective before any retrieval-specific fine-tuning, explicitly designed to produce embeddings well suited to downstream passage retrieval. This thesis evaluates whether that retrieval-specific pretraining transfers to a domain (company profiles) quite different from the passage-retrieval benchmarks it was designed for (Section~\ref{sec:simlm}).

\section{Hybrid Retrieval, Reranking, and Learning to Rank}
\label{sec:lit_hybrid}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Sparse and dense retrieval, and different dense models, make different errors. A hybrid system that combines multiple retrieval signals can recover from a single method's individual weaknesses. Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) \citep{cormack2009reciprocal} is a simple, training-free method for combining several ranked lists. Each document's fused score is computed as the sum of $1/(k_{\mathrm{RRF}} + \mathrm{rank})$ across the lists in which it appears, requiring only that documents be ranked rather than that their raw scores be comparable across methods. RRF underlies the hybrid baseline in Section~\ref{sec:rrf}.

A complementary technique is \emph{reranking}. A first-stage retriever returns a candidate set cheaply, and a more expensive model reorders it. Cross-encoder rerankers, such as the \texttt{bge-reranker-v2-m3} \citep{baaireranker2024} and \texttt{mxbai-rerank-large-v1} \citep{mixedbread2024mxbai} models used in Sections~\ref{sec:hybrid_fullpool} and~\ref{sec:reranker_ablation}, feed the query and each candidate into the same transformer jointly, rather than encoding them independently as a bi-encoder does. This lets the model attend directly between query and candidate tokens, typically producing more accurate relevance scores than a bi-encoder \citep{nogueira2019passage}, at the cost of requiring a full forward pass per candidate rather than a single fast vector comparison, so cross-encoders are used to rerank a small candidate set rather than to search a full collection.

More generally, combining multiple retrieval signals into a single relevance score can be framed as \emph{learning to rank} \citep{liu2009learning}. Instead of a fixed, hand-designed combination rule such as RRF, a model is trained directly on labelled query-document pairs to learn how to weight and combine whatever features are available (similarity scores, ranks, or other signals) into a final ranking. This is the approach taken by the learned fusion ranker in Section~\ref{sec:learned_fusion}, implemented with the logistic regression and gradient-boosted tree models provided by scikit-learn \citep{pedregosa2011scikit}.

\section{Company and Entity-Level Retrieval}
\label{sec:lit_entity}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Much of the retrieval literature reviewed above is developed and evaluated on general-purpose passage and document retrieval benchmarks, questions answered from Wikipedia or news text. Comparatively little of it addresses retrieval over company-profile data specifically. The applied literature closest to this thesis's own task instead comes from \emph{entity matching} and \emph{entity resolution}. Both address the problem of deciding whether two records, often company or product records drawn from different sources, refer to the same real-world entity. DeepMatcher \citep{mudgal2018deep} was among the first to systematically apply deep learning to this problem, and Ditto \citep{li2020deep} later showed that fine-tuning a pretrained language model on the same task, framed as a sequence-pair classification problem, achieves very high accuracy, over 96\% F1 on company-matching benchmarks specifically. More recently, \citet{peeters2025entity} show that large language models, prompted directly rather than fine-tuned for the task, can match or exceed these earlier approaches on the same entity matching benchmarks. Entity matching and the retrieval task addressed in this thesis are related but distinct. Matching decides whether two already-identified records are the same entity, a pairwise classification problem, while retrieval must first find, from a natural-language query, which of millions of candidate companies are even worth considering, a search problem over an entire corpus rather than a single pair.

Closer still to this thesis's own setting is Company2Vec \citep{gerling2023company}, which builds company embeddings directly from the unstructured text of corporate websites, the same kind of web-derived company description this thesis's own AI-generated summaries are built from, and uses those embeddings to identify similar companies for peer-firm and industry-classification tasks. Company2Vec demonstrates that web-derived company text carries a usable semantic signal for retrieval-adjacent tasks, but it does not address ranked retrieval against a natural-language query, evaluate against a validated relevance standard, or test at the scale addressed in this thesis. This is precisely the gap this thesis fills. General-purpose semantic retrieval is well studied, and company-level embeddings have been shown to work for adjacent tasks like matching and peer-firm identification. The literature reviewed for this thesis did not identify prior work evaluating full retrieval, from a natural-language query to a ranked set of company candidates, on data and at a scale comparable to this thesis's own, under an evaluation protocol whose own validity has been separately established.

\section{Evaluation Without Human Relevance Judgements}
\label{sec:lit_evaluation}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Even once a retrieval task and domain are defined, evaluating a system against it requires a way to decide which results are actually relevant. Standard information retrieval evaluation compares each system's ranking against a set of human relevance judgements for a fixed query set, historically constructed through pooling. In pooling, the top results from several systems are merged and judged by human assessors, as established by the methodology of the Text REtrieval Conference (TREC) \citep{voorhees2002philosophy}. No such human-annotated relevance judgements exist for the company search task addressed in this thesis. In their absence, this thesis instead adopts a \emph{pseudo-relevance} protocol (Section~\ref{subsec:eval_protocol}). Under this protocol, a company is treated as relevant if it appears in the existing production system's own results for that query. This provides a practical way to compare systems when human judgements are unavailable, although the resulting scores measure agreement with the reference system rather than absolute relevance, a limitation this thesis states explicitly wherever pseudo-relevance labels are used. Separately, the BEIR benchmark \citep{thakur2021beir} demonstrates the value of evaluating retrieval methods across diverse tasks and domains using established relevance assessments, rather than relying on a single retrieval task or domain.

A more recent alternative to human pooling, and the one this thesis relies on for its independent silver standard (Section~\ref{subsec:pooled_gold_standard}), is to use large language models themselves as relevance judges. \citet{thomas2024llmjudge} show that LLM judgments can be useful for large-scale system comparison, but agreement with human judgement, prediction of user preference, and agreement between LLM judges are related, distinct claims, and reliability on any of them depends on the task, the prompting procedure, and the judge model used. Published findings on one task or judge pairing therefore cannot simply be assumed to transfer to this thesis's own. This is exactly why this thesis's own blind calibration check (Section~\ref{subsec:pooled_gold_standard_result}) tests agreement directly against its own judges and task, rather than relying on the literature's findings alone. This approach is not without open problems. A broader survey of LLM-as-a-judge methods \citep{li2024fromgeneration} documents recurring failure modes across the wider literature, including inconsistency across repeated runs, sensitivity to the order candidates are presented in, and systematic scoring biases that do not necessarily show up in simple percent-agreement figures, part of the motivation for this thesis's use of Cohen's kappa rather than raw agreement alone (Section~\ref{subsec:pooled_gold_standard_result}) and for cross-checking judges from different model families rather than relying on a single model's judgments throughout. Because these labels are produced through a model-assisted, human-adjudicated process rather than exhaustive expert annotation, this thesis refers to them throughout as a \textbf{silver standard} rather than a gold standard, a distinction discussed further in Section~\ref{subsec:pooled_gold_standard}.

\paragraph{The silver standard should be read with caution, not treated as ground truth.} Despite the name, the silver standard is not a gold standard, and every result evaluated against it in this thesis should be read accordingly. It rests on LLM judgments with only minimal, and not fully independent, human checking. The author personally resolves judge disagreements (roughly 16\% of candidates), and those same resolved labels then score the author's own retrieval methods, a conflict of interest that is disclosed and partially mitigated but not eliminated (Section~\ref{subsec:honest_synthesis} discusses this in full). The candidates it covers were also not drawn randomly. Active-learning sampling deliberately targets ambiguous, boundary-case candidates for labelling (Section~\ref{subsec:active_learning}), so the standard is systematically skewed toward harder cases rather than a representative sample of all relevant companies. \section{Phase I: Baselines}
\label{sec:phase1_method}

\subsection{Dataset}
\label{subsec:dataset}

The evaluation corpus is derived from Istari's production search logs. A set of 101 representative queries was provided by Istari, covering a wide range of company search intents, including industry-specific queries (e.g.\ \textit{``pharmaceutical companies in the US''}), geographic queries (e.g.\ \textit{``tech startups in Berlin''}), and functional queries (e.g.\ \textit{``B2B SaaS companies''}). For each query, the production system returned its top-1\,000 ranked results, yielding 101\,000 query--company pairs in total. After de-duplication across queries, the corpus consists of \textbf{98\,716 unique companies}, each represented by a domain name, a company name, and an AI-generated natural-language summary of the company's business activity.

The summary field was selected as the primary retrieval field after an exploratory data analysis of all available GOI attributes. It was found to be fully populated across the corpus (0\% missing values) and to contain the richest semantic content for matching against free-text queries, compared to structured fields such as industry codes or founding year. This field is not without its own data-quality issues, however. For a subset of query topics, the summary instead describes a website hosting or platform provider rather than the company's own business, an issue quantified directly in Section~\ref{sec:trust_filter}.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{subsec:eval_protocol}

\paragraph{Pseudo-relevance labels.} No human-annotated relevance judgements are available for the Istari company search task. Following standard practice in information retrieval research when ground-truth labels are absent~\citep{voorhees2002philosophy}, this thesis adopts a \textit{pseudo-relevance} assumption: a company is considered \textbf{relevant} for a given query if and only if it appears in the \textbf{top-1\,000 results} of Istari's production search system for that query. This yields a relevance set of 1\,000 companies per query, representing ${\sim}1\%$ of the total corpus. This thesis deliberately chooses this threshold over a stricter one so that reranking, fusion, and oracle-ceiling experiments later in this chapter have enough relevant companies per query to produce meaningful comparisons over large candidate pools.

This assumption has an important implication for the evaluation: the metrics measure the degree to which each retrieval system agrees with the production system, not whether it recovers objectively relevant companies. Consequently, a retrieval system that is strictly better than the production system may appear to underperform because it surfaces companies the production system missed. This limitation is acknowledged throughout the analysis.

\paragraph{Metrics.} Three standard information retrieval metrics are reported at cut-offs $k \in \{10, 50, 100, 1000\}$:

Writing $R_q$ for the set of relevant companies for query $q$ and $T_k(q)$ for the top-$k$ companies a system retrieves for $q$:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Precision@$k$}: the fraction of the top-$k$ retrieved companies that are relevant, $\text{Precision@}k = \frac{|T_k(q) \cap R_q|}{k}$. This measures user-facing quality: what a user sees on the first page of results.

\item \textbf{Recall@$k$}: the fraction of all relevant companies that appear in the top-$k$ results, $\text{Recall@}k = \frac{|T_k(q) \cap R_q|}{|R_q|}$. This measures coverage, important for downstream re-ranking pipelines that operate on a retrieved candidate set. \textbf{Recall is the primary metric in this thesis}, following the priority set by Istari for this task: whether the system finds the relevant companies matters more than the exact order they are ranked in. Section~\ref{subsec:quality} explains how Precision@$k$ and Recall@$k$ relate to one another and should be read together.

\item \textbf{NDCG@$k$} (Normalised Discounted Cumulative Gain): a ranking-aware metric that rewards systems for placing relevant companies higher in the list, using a logarithmic discount. NDCG@$k$ is reported as a secondary, supporting metric: it reflects the experience of a user who interacts primarily with the top results, which is a reasonable concern in practice even though it is not the priority metric for this thesis.
\end{itemize}

All metrics are averaged across all 101 queries. Precision and Recall are always reported at the same cut-off values so that the precision--recall trade-off at each depth is visible.

\paragraph{Reading Precision and Recall together.} Every one of the 101 queries has exactly 1\,000 relevant companies under the pseudo-relevance protocol. This has a direct consequence for how Precision@$k$ and Recall@$k$ relate to one another at any cut-off $k \le 1\,000$: since both metrics are computed from the same count (how many of the top-$k$ retrieved companies are relevant), just divided by a different denominator, \[ \text{Recall@}k = \text{Precision@}k \times \frac{k}{1\,000}, \] so the two metrics are exactly proportional to one another at every cut-off, and neither is redundant on its own: Precision@$k$ answers ``of what the system returned, how much was correct'', while Recall@$k$ answers ``of everything that is correct, how much did the system return'', and the two only coincide once $k = 1\,000$, where the denominators become equal. A practical consequence is that Recall@$k$ for small $k$ can look misleadingly low in isolation: a system with an excellent Precision@10 of, say, 0.97 (see Table~\ref{tab:main_results} in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} for the actual measured values) will still show a Recall@10 of only $0.97 \times 10/1\,000 \approx 0.0097$, not because the top-10 results are poor, but because 10 items can never cover more than 1\% of a 1\,000-company relevant set. For this reason, Precision@$k$ is the more directly interpretable number at shallow cut-offs, while Recall@1\,000 is treated as the headline metric throughout this thesis, since $k=1\,000$ is the one cut-off at which Recall stops being a rescaled copy of Precision and becomes a genuinely independent measure of total coverage.

\label{subsec:quality}
\paragraph{Computational environment.}
\label{subsec:compute}

All experiments are conducted on bwUniCluster 3.0, a shared high-performance computing (HPC) cluster accessed through the University of Mannheim.\footnote{\url{https://wiki.bwhpc.de/e/BwUniCluster3.0}} Dense model encoding is performed on an NVIDIA A100 graphics processing unit (GPU) with 80\,GB HBM2 memory, on a node equipped with two Intel Xeon Platinum 8358 CPUs (64 physical cores total). BM25 retrieval runs on the central processing unit (CPU) instead, since it does not involve any neural network computation. Query latency measurements are performed on the same hardware and reported as the mean over 20 benchmark queries to obtain stable estimates. Latency is decomposed into three components (local query encoding, FAISS index search, and external network time) so that self-hosted and API-based methods are compared on equal terms.

\subsection{Sparse Retrieval Baseline: BM25}
\label{sec:baseline_sparse}

\textbf{BM25} is a classic \emph{lexical} (keyword-matching) retrieval algorithm: it scores each candidate document by how often the query's exact terms appear in it, weighted so that rare, distinctive terms count for more than common ones. It requires no training and no semantic understanding of the text, matching is based purely on shared vocabulary between query and document. It is included here as the sparse-retrieval baseline against which every \emph{dense} (embedding-based, semantic) method in this chapter is compared.
\label{subsec:bm25_impl}

The BM25 baseline is implemented with the \texttt{rank\_bm25} Python library~\citep{robertson2009probabilistic}, chosen for this thesis specifically because it provides the Okapi BM25 variant with well-established default hyperparameters ($k_1 = 1.5$, $b = 0.75$), avoiding the need for a separate hyperparameter search for what is only the lexical baseline. All 98\,716 company summaries are tokenised using the punkt tokeniser from the Natural Language Toolkit (NLTK)~\citep{nltk} with lower-casing applied, and the resulting token lists are used to construct the BM25 inverted index. At query time, each query is tokenised identically and scored against the index. The top-1\,000 companies by BM25 score are retrieved for each query and stored for evaluation. Index construction takes 28.7 seconds and requires no GPU.

\subsection{Dense Retrieval Baselines}
\label{sec:baseline_dense}

\textbf{Dense retrieval} represents both the query and every candidate document as a fixed-length numerical vector (an \emph{embedding}) produced by a neural network, such that texts with similar meaning end up as nearby vectors in that space, regardless of whether they share any exact words. This directly targets BM25's weakness: two texts can be semantically related while sharing almost no vocabulary. Three dense retrieval baselines are evaluated first, covering a range of model sizes, computational requirements, and deployment constraints. All three follow the same two-stage pipeline introduced by \citet{karpukhin2020dense}: (i) encode all company summaries offline into a dense vector index using FAISS~\citep{johnson2017billion}, and (ii) at query time, encode the query into a vector and retrieve the top-1\,000 nearest neighbours from the index. These three models are chosen for this first comparison specifically because they are widely deployed, well-understood reference points spanning small self-hosted (MiniLM), large self-hosted (BGE), and commercial API (OpenAI) deployment profiles, the kind of choice a practitioner actually weighs, rather than an attempt to benchmark the newest available models; Section~\ref{sec:extended_baselines} broadens this comparison to eight further models, including several released after this initial comparison was run, specifically to check whether a newer or larger model changes the conclusion.

\subsubsection{MiniLM (all-MiniLM-L6-v2)}
\label{subsec:minilm}

\texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} is a lightweight sentence-transformer model~\citep{reimers2019sentence} with 22 million parameters, producing 384-dimensional embeddings. It is distilled from a larger BERT model and fine-tuned on sentence similarity tasks. All 98\,716 company summaries are encoded in a single batch pass, taking 0.6 minutes on the A100 GPU. A \texttt{faiss.IndexFlatL2} index (exact Euclidean search) is constructed over the resulting embeddings in 0.2 seconds. MiniLM does not require embedding normalisation.

MiniLM is included as the smallest and most computationally efficient dense baseline, providing a lower bound on dense retrieval quality and establishing whether a minimal dense model is sufficient to outperform BM25.

\subsubsection{BGE (bge-large-en-v1.5)}
\label{subsec:bge}

\begin{sloppypar}
\texttt{BAAI/bge-large-en-v1.5} is a 335-million parameter BERT-large model developed by the Beijing Academy of Artificial Intelligence~\citep{xiao2024cpack}, producing 1\,024-dimensional embeddings. It is specifically optimised for dense retrieval through hard negative mining during fine-tuning, a training strategy in which the model is exposed to query--document pairs that are superficially similar but semantically distinct, forcing it to learn fine-grained semantic distinctions.
\end{sloppypar}

Encoding all 98\,716 company summaries takes 7.0 minutes on the A100 GPU at a batch size of 256. BGE requires L2 normalisation of all embeddings (\texttt{normalize\_embeddings=True}), as it is trained under the cosine similarity objective. Accordingly, a \texttt{faiss.IndexFlatIP} index (inner product, equivalent to cosine similarity for unit-normalised vectors) is used rather than \texttt{IndexFlatL2}.

At query time, each query is encoded independently and normalised before being passed to the FAISS index; the resulting query latency is reported alongside every other method's in Section~\ref{sec:exp_runtime_result}.

\subsubsection{OpenAI (text-embedding-3-small)}
\label{subsec:openai}

\texttt{text-embedding-3-small} is a commercial embedding model provided by OpenAI via their REST API. It produces 1\,536-dimensional embeddings. Unlike the open-source models above, the architecture and training procedure are proprietary. Encoding all 98\,716 company summaries is performed via batched API calls (batch size 500) and takes 9.7 minutes, of which the majority is network latency rather than model computation. The resulting embeddings are unit-normalised by the API, and a \texttt{faiss.IndexFlatIP} index is constructed locally. The total cost of encoding the full corpus is approximately \$0.15 at the published pricing of \$0.02 per million tokens.

This model is included as the commercial upper-bound baseline, testing whether a high-quality API-based embedding model outperforms the open-source alternatives evaluated above.

\section{Phase II: Improving Retrieval}
\label{sec:phase2_method}

\subsection{Extended Baseline Comparison: Additional Embedding Models}
\label{sec:extended_baselines}

Section~\ref{sec:baseline_dense}'s three dense baselines already span a size and cost spectrum, from MiniLM's 22 million parameters to OpenAI's paid commercial API, and found the much smaller MiniLM ahead of both BGE and OpenAI, which were themselves effectively tied with one another. This raises a direct follow-up question with real practical stakes for a production deployment: does retrieval quality keep improving as model size and specialisation increase further, and if so, is that improvement worth the added compute cost, or does quality plateau while cost keeps climbing? Five further dense embedding models were evaluated to test this, on the same 98\,716-company corpus, all-fields text representation, and 101-query evaluation set described in Section~\ref{subsec:dataset}, deliberately split into two tiers. The first tier stays close to BGE's scale (1\,024-dimensional models, comparable parameter count): BGE-M3 and GTE-large. The second tier jumps to 7-billion-parameter models, an order of magnitude larger, but holds the base architecture and parameter count fixed across all three (E5-Mistral-7B-Instruct, Linq-Embed-Mistral, SFR-Embedding-Mistral all build on the same Mistral-7B backbone), varying only their fine-tuning data and objective, so any quality difference among these three specifically isolates the effect of fine-tuning from the effect of raw scale, rather than confounding the two:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{BGE-M3} (\texttt{BAAI/bge-m3}): a multi-lingual, multi-functionality embedding model, 1\,024-dimensional embeddings.
\item \textbf{GTE-large} (\texttt{thenlper/gte-large}): a general-purpose text embedding model, 1\,024 dimensions.
\item \textbf{E5-Mistral-7B-Instruct} (\texttt{intfloat/e5-mistral-7b-instruct}): an instruction-tuned 7-billion-parameter Mistral-based embedding model, 4\,096 dimensions.
\item \textbf{Linq-Embed-Mistral} (\texttt{Linq-AI-Research/Linq-Embed-Mistral}): a 7B-parameter Mistral-based embedding model fine-tuned for retrieval, 4\,096 dimensions.
\item \textbf{SFR-Embedding-Mistral} (\texttt{Salesforce/SFR-Embedding-Mistral}): Salesforce's 7B-parameter Mistral-based retrieval embedding model, 4\,096 dimensions.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Query expansion: HyDE pipeline.}
\label{sec:hyde}

\textbf{HyDE} (Hypothetical Document Embeddings) is a query-expansion technique: instead of embedding the user's short query directly, an LLM first generates several hypothetical, ideal-answer documents for that query, and their embeddings are averaged into a single, richer query vector. This thesis implements HyDE by asking a local LLM (\texttt{llama3.1:8b} via Ollama) to generate $N=10$ stochastic (temperature~$=0.8$) hypothetical company descriptions that would ideally answer the query. The embeddings of these 10 descriptions are averaged into a single query vector, which is then searched against the MiniLM company index. A second stage reranks the top-50 HyDE results with a cross-encoder (\texttt{BAAI/bge-reranker-v2-m3}).

\paragraph{Hybrid retrieval: Reciprocal Rank Fusion.}
\label{sec:rrf}

\textbf{Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF)} combines two or more separately produced rankings into one, by giving every document a score based only on its \emph{rank position} in each input ranking (not its raw similarity score, which is not directly comparable across different retrieval methods), then summing those rank-based scores across methods:
\[
\mathrm{RRF}(d) = \sum_{\text{method}} \frac{1}{k_{\mathrm{RRF}} + \mathrm{rank}(d)}
\]
As an alternative to query expansion, BM25 and MiniLM rankings are fused directly using RRF with $k_{\mathrm{RRF}}=60$ \citep{cormack2009reciprocal}. A second stage reranks the top-50 fused results with the same cross-encoder used for HyDE.

\paragraph{ANN index scaling to 2 million vectors.}
\label{sec:ann_scaling}

\textbf{Approximate Nearest Neighbour (ANN) search} trades a small amount of retrieval accuracy for a large speed-up, by searching an index structure that does not guarantee finding the exact closest vectors, unlike the exact brute-force search (\texttt{IndexFlatL2}/\texttt{IndexFlatIP}) used everywhere else in this chapter so far. This section runs a first, synthetic-scale test of that trade-off. To test whether the exact search used throughout this chapter (\texttt{IndexFlatL2}/\texttt{IndexFlatIP}) remains practical at production scale, the 98\,716 real MiniLM company embeddings were padded with 1\,901\,284 synthetic filler vectors (real embeddings perturbed with Gaussian noise at 15\% of the average vector norm), giving a 2-million-vector corpus. Exact search (\texttt{FlatL2}) was compared against approximate indexes: IVF (\texttt{nlist}=4096, with \texttt{nprobe} $\in \{1, 8, 32, 128, 4096\}$) and HNSW ($M=32$, with \texttt{efSearch} $\in \{16, 64, 256\}$).

\paragraph{Recall@1000 error analysis.}
\label{sec:error_analysis}

To understand why exact MiniLM search caps at Recall@1000~=~0.741 (Section~\ref{sec:ann_scaling}), every production-relevant company that MiniLM failed to retrieve within the top-1\,000 was extracted and searched for at much greater depth ($k=10\,000$), yielding 26\,185 missed (query, company) pairs across all 101 queries.

\subsection{Corpus Quality: Platform Boilerplate in AI-Generated Summaries}
\label{sec:trust_filter}

The duplicated ``Bluehost.com'' description found in Section~\ref{sec:error_analysis} is one instance of a broader, previously unquantified data-quality issue affecting the \texttt{summary} field that every retrieval method in this thesis depends on. For a meaningful share of companies in the corpus, the AI-generated summary describes the website hosting provider or website-building platform that the company's domain happens to run on, rather than the company's own business. This happens because many small businesses have a thin, template-driven web presence, so the process that generates each company's summary from its web content sometimes picks up generic platform language (for example, marketing copy for a web hosting provider or an e-commerce storefront builder) instead of any information specific to the company itself. A company using such a platform is then, in effect, indistinguishable in its summary text from every other company using the same platform, which is precisely the mechanism behind the large-scale description duplication already observed in Section~\ref{sec:error_analysis}.

\paragraph{Detection approach.} A classifier was built to flag boilerplate summaries automatically, using embedding similarity between each company's summary and a small, fixed reference set of around 300 known boilerplate templates, an approach chosen so that classifying any single company costs one embedding comparison against a fixed-size reference set, regardless of whether the underlying corpus is the 98\,716-company evaluation set used in this thesis or the full multi-million-company GOI.

\paragraph{Oracle union recall ceiling.}
\label{sec:oracle_union}

Before investing in a trained reranker or a fine-tuning pipeline, it is useful to know the best-case recall achievable by simply combining the candidate pools already produced by every method run so far, at zero additional encoding cost, since every channel's top-1\,000 results per query are already cached on disk. This experiment computes, for every combination of the six available channels (MiniLM, BM25, BGE-all-fields, BGE-summary, OpenAI-large, Nomic), the recall of the \emph{union} of their top-1\,000 candidate sets against the top-1\,000 ground truth.

\subsection{SimLM: A Purpose-Built Retrieval Pre-training Baseline}
\label{sec:simlm}

All dense baselines evaluated so far are general-purpose sentence-embedding models. \textbf{SimLM} \citep{wang2022simlm} takes a different approach: it is a BERT-base (110\,M parameter) encoder pre-trained specifically for the retrieval task itself, matching a short query to a longer passage via a representation-bottleneck objective, before any task-specific fine-tuning. It is included to test whether a model purpose-built for retrieval, but far smaller than the 7B-parameter models in Section~\ref{sec:extended_baselines}, can match or exceed them.

\paragraph{Implementation.} The public checkpoint \texttt{intfloat/simlm-base-msmarco-finetuned} is used, following its documented convention rather than the all-fields concatenation used elsewhere in this thesis: the company name is encoded as a \emph{title}, and the remaining fields (country, state, city, type, size, industry, summary, keywords) as the paired \emph{body} text, using CLS-token pooling (not mean pooling) with L2 normalisation. Queries are encoded without any prefix, following the same model card.

\subsection{ColBERT: Late-Interaction Retrieval via Brute-Force MaxSim}
\label{sec:colbert}

Every method evaluated so far represents each company and each query as a single dense vector. \textbf{ColBERT} \citep{khattab2020colbert, santhanam2022colbertv2} instead represents both as \emph{sets of per-token} embeddings and scores a (query, company) pair using \emph{MaxSim}: for every query token, the maximum similarity to any company token is taken, and these per-token maxima are summed. This \emph{late-interaction} design is architecturally richer than single-vector dense retrieval, since no single fixed-size vector has to compress the entire meaning of a company description, and it is included to test whether that added expressiveness translates into higher recall on this task.

\paragraph{Implementation.} The public checkpoint \texttt{colbert-ir/colbertv2.0} is used, encoding each company summary into a sequence of 128-dimensional per-token embeddings via the model's linear projection layer. Due to indexing and library constraints on the cluster used for this thesis, the official PLAID-indexed ColBERT pipeline was not used; instead, MaxSim scoring is implemented from scratch as a brute-force computation over all 98\,716 companies for every query. This is computationally more expensive than an indexed implementation would be, but is exact rather than approximate, and is tractable at this corpus size.

\subsection{Hybrid Full-Pool Reranking}
\label{sec:hybrid_fullpool}

Section~\ref{sec:rrf} showed that fusing BM25 and MiniLM with Reciprocal Rank Fusion, followed by reranking only the top-50 fused candidates, essentially reproduces MiniLM's own quality (NDCG@10~=~0.975) without improving Recall@1000. Section~\ref{sec:oracle_union} then showed that the \emph{union} of several channels' top-1\,000 candidate pools reaches a much higher oracle recall ceiling than any single channel. Together, these two findings motivate a different hybrid design: rerank the \emph{entire} union pool of several dense channels with a cross-encoder, rather than fusing rankings and reranking only a shallow, fixed-size slice of the result.

\paragraph{Implementation.} For each query, the top-1\,000 results from a set of dense channels are combined by simple set union (no rank fusion, no truncation), giving an unranked candidate pool that is typically 1\,300--1\,800 companies. A cross-encoder reranker, \texttt{BAAI/bge-reranker-v2-m3} \citep{baaireranker2024}, then scores every (query, candidate) pair in the full pool directly, and the top-1\,000 by reranker score are kept as the final result. Four variants are compared, adding one channel at a time:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{2-way}: MiniLM~+~Linq-Embed-Mistral.
\item \textbf{3-way}: 2-way~+~GTE-large.
\item \textbf{4-way}: 3-way~+~OpenAI-large.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Reranker ablation: isolating the bottleneck.}
\label{sec:reranker_ablation}

Given the channel-saturation finding in Section~\ref{sec:hybrid_fullpool}, the natural next question is whether the specific reranker model used throughout this chapter, \texttt{BAAI/bge-reranker-v2-m3}, is itself limiting recall, or whether reranker choice matters little once the candidate pool is fixed.

\paragraph{Implementation.} \texttt{BAAI/bge-reranker-v2-m3} (built on an XLM-RoBERTa backbone) is replaced with \texttt{mixedbread-ai/mxbai-rerank-large-v1} \citep{mixedbread2024mxbai} (built on a DeBERTa-v2 backbone, an architecturally distinct, comparably-sized cross-encoder), applied to the \emph{exact same} 3-way union pool from Section~\ref{sec:hybrid_fullpool}. Only the reranker model changes; the candidate pool, corpus, and queries are identical.

\subsection{Learned Fusion Ranker}
\label{sec:learned_fusion}

Every hybrid pipeline so far combines \emph{frozen}, independently pretrained components: candidate lists are pooled by simple union, and a pretrained cross-encoder, never fit to this task's own queries or labels, reranks the result. This section instead treats fusion as a supervised \textbf{learning-to-rank} problem: a model is trained directly on this thesis's own 101 queries and their pseudo-relevance labels to learn how to combine multiple retrieval signals, rather than relying on a fixed, hand-designed combination rule.

\paragraph{Formal setup.} For each (query, candidate) pair, the model receives a ten-dimensional input feature vector $x \in \mathbb{R}^{10}$ (the similarity scores, inverse ranks, reranker score, and channel count described below) and predicts a single output $\hat{y} \in [0, 1]$, the estimated probability that the candidate is relevant to the query. The training target $y \in \{0, 1\}$ is the pseudo-relevance label from Section~\ref{subsec:eval_protocol} (1 if the candidate is in production's top-1\,000 for that query, 0 otherwise). Both model variants are trained to minimise binary cross-entropy (log loss), $\mathcal{L}(y, \hat{y}) = -\left[y \log \hat{y} + (1 - y) \log (1 - \hat{y})\right]$, averaged over all training (query, candidate) pairs, the standard objective for a probabilistic binary classifier and the default loss for both \texttt{scikit-learn} estimators used here.

\paragraph{Implementation.} For every (query, candidate) pair in the same 3-way union pool used in Section~\ref{sec:hybrid_fullpool} (with BM25 added as a fourth, lexical, signal), ten features are computed: the raw similarity score and inverse rank ($1/\text{rank}$) from each of MiniLM, Linq-Embed-Mistral, GTE-large, and BM25; the \texttt{BAAI/bge-reranker-v2-m3} score from Section~\ref{sec:hybrid_fullpool} (included as one input feature among ten, not as the final decision rule); and a count of how many channels retrieved the candidate at all. Two models are trained on these features using \texttt{scikit-learn} \citep{pedregosa2011scikit}: a logistic regression, and a histogram-based gradient-boosted tree ensemble. Crucially, both are trained and evaluated using 5-fold cross-validation grouped by \texttt{query\_id}: for each fold, the model is fit on candidates from 80\% of queries and scores held-out candidates from the remaining 20\%, so that no query is ever scored by a model that has seen its own label during training. Final metrics are computed by concatenating the held-out predictions from all five folds, giving a complete, leakage-free evaluation over all 101 queries.

\section{Phase III: Evaluation Validity}
\label{sec:phase3_method}

The pseudo-relevance protocol used throughout this thesis (Section~\ref{subsec:eval_protocol}) treats a company as relevant if and only if it appears in production's own results for a query. This is a reasonable practice when no human-annotated relevance judgements exist (Section~\ref{sec:lit_evaluation}), but it carries a specific risk for the learned fusion ranker (Section~\ref{sec:learned_fusion}): a model trained directly on labels derived from production's own output may partly be learning to reproduce whatever idiosyncrasies that particular system has, rather than genuine relevance. This section reports a small-scale attempt to check whether the learned fusion ranker's key interpretive finding, that MiniLM's inverse rank is the most informative feature, survives replacing that circular ground truth with an independently constructed one.

\subsection{Building a Pooled, Independently Judged Silver Standard}
\label{subsec:pooled_gold_standard}

This section builds this thesis's \emph{silver standard}\footnote{This thesis calls the pooled, multi-judge, human-tie-broken labels built in this section a \emph{silver standard} rather than a \emph{gold standard}, since LLM judges with only minimal human checking do not meet the bar traditional IR evaluation reserves for that term. The larger, classifier-distilled set built from it in Section~\ref{sec:silver_labels} is correspondingly renamed from \emph{silver} to \emph{bronze}, one trust tier below. The underlying code and data files use \texttt{gold\_label} as the literal column name throughout; this naming choice predates the silver/bronze terminology introduced here and was kept as-is in the code.}, a pooled, multi-judge, human-adjudicated relevance standard used throughout the remainder of this thesis.

\paragraph{Pooling.} Following the pooling methodology established for the Text REtrieval Conference \citep{voorhees2002philosophy, zobel1998reliable}, the top-20 candidates per query were taken from six sources: the frozen production ground truth, a freshly fetched sample from Istari's live v2 search API, BM25, GTE-large, ColBERT, and the learned fusion ranker's own output, then unioned and deduplicated by domain. Applied across all 101 queries, this yields 8\,309 pooled candidates, an average of 82.3 unique candidates per query, a substantially smaller and more tractable set than the original 1\,000-per-query pool.

\paragraph{Trust filtering.} Before any relevance judging, every pooled candidate's summary was checked against the boilerplate detector from Section~\ref{sec:trust_filter}, reusing its already-cached reference templates. This flagged 487 of 8\,309 candidates (5.9\%) as untrustworthy, which were excluded from judging entirely, so that a summary describing the wrong entity could not poison a relevance label built on top of it.

\paragraph{Multi-judge relevance labelling.} Each remaining, trustworthy candidate was independently judged by two large language models (OpenAI's \texttt{gpt-4o-mini} and Anthropic's Claude Haiku), on a three-level scale (0~=~not relevant, 1~=~partially relevant, 2~=~highly relevant), following the LLM-as-judge approach validated at scale by \citet{thomas2024llmjudge}. These two smaller, cheaper models were chosen deliberately for this stage rather than the strongest available models at the time: the initial pilot and subsequent active-learning expansion (Section~\ref{subsec:active_learning}) required judging over a thousand candidates, and a lower per-candidate cost was what made judging at that volume affordable at all. This choice is validated, not merely assumed to be adequate, in two ways: the blind calibration check immediately below, and a direct kappa comparison against a stronger judge pair (Section~\ref{subsec:pooled_gold_standard_result}), which confirms the cheaper pair reaches ``substantial'' rather than ``almost perfect'' agreement, a real but bounded gap that motivated upgrading to stronger judges (\texttt{claude-sonnet-5}, \texttt{gpt-5.4}, and Google's Gemini) for the later spot-checks and the production-independent evaluation, where per-candidate accuracy mattered more than judging volume (Sections~\ref{sec:silver_labels_result}, \ref{subsec:production_independent_eval}). Where the two judges agreed, that label was accepted directly; where they disagreed, the case was resolved by manual review, performed by the author. The resulting standard is therefore independent of the production system, but not fully independent of the researcher; this limitation is discussed in full, along with the steps taken to mitigate it, in Section~\ref{subsec:honest_synthesis}.

\paragraph{Blind calibration of the judges.} Resolving disagreements by reading both judges' stated reasoning risks anchoring the human reviewer toward whichever judge's framing is read first. To check this, a separate blind sample of 20 candidates that neither judge had yet seen was labelled by the author with no access to either judge's output, then scored independently by both judges. OpenAI matched the blind label 75\% of the time and Claude 80\%, close enough that neither judge should be treated as categorically more reliable than the other on this task.

\paragraph{Scope of this pilot.} Labelling all 101 queries with two paid LLM judges was not pursued in the time available; instead, a 5-query pilot was completed first. Of 430 candidates judged, 360 (83.7\%) received unanimous agreement between the two judges. Of the remaining 70 disagreements, 59 were resolved by manual review and 11 were excluded outright for insufficient evidence (for example, unreachable domains or placeholder websites with no verifiable content), rather than being forced into a label the available information could not support. This yields \textbf{419 silver-labelled candidates} across 5 queries, distributed as 8 not relevant, 119 partially relevant, and 292 highly relevant.

\subsection{Retraining Against the New Silver Standard}
\label{subsec:refit_fusion_ranker}

The exact architecture from Section~\ref{sec:learned_fusion} (the same ten features, the same 5-fold cross-validation grouped by \texttt{query\_id}, here equivalent to leave-one-query-out since exactly five queries are covered) was retrained using the 417 of 419 silver-labelled candidates that matched an existing feature row from the original candidate pool. The binary classification target used the strict threshold \texttt{gold\_label} = 2 (highly relevant only, 291 positive vs.\ 126 negative examples); the more permissive threshold \texttt{gold\_label} $\geq$ 1 leaves only 8 negative examples across the whole pilot, too few for cross-validated coefficients to be estimated reliably.

\label{sec:silver_labels}

No gold-standard relevance data exists for this task at any scale to build on, Istari's full 20-million-company database has never been exhaustively human-labelled by anyone, so this thesis treats labelling as an incremental process rather than a one-time effort: verify a small, independently judged set first (Section~\ref{subsec:pooled_gold_standard}), then decide, concretely, how much further it is affordable and worthwhile to extend it, rather than assuming the answer is either ``all of it'' or ``none of it''. Extending independent LLM judging to every candidate the retrieval systems in this thesis actually produce is not affordable. This estimate is not a guess: it scales the per-candidate API cost actually incurred while judging the 419-candidate pilot with two LLM judges (Section~\ref{subsec:pooled_gold_standard}) up to larger volumes at different pooling depths. The original fusion ranker's own candidate pool (Section~\ref{sec:learned_fusion}) covers 173\,262 (query, candidate) pairs across all 101 queries; judging every one of those at the pilot's observed per-candidate cost would cost approximately \$22\,000 at the current pooling depth, rising toward \$28\,000 at a deeper pooling depth approaching exhaustive judging of every one of the 98\,716 corpus companies against every one of the 101 queries. The latter figure is not just expensive but methodologically wasteful: the overwhelming majority of those pairs are companies that no retrieval channel ever considered plausible for that query, judging them would mean paying to confirm the obvious.

\paragraph{Approach.} Instead, the 419 silver-labelled candidates from Section~\ref{subsec:pooled_gold_standard} are used to train a final classifier (the same architecture and feature set as Section~\ref{sec:learned_fusion}, fit on all 419 labels rather than held out via cross-validation, since this model is intended for deployment rather than evaluation). This classifier is then applied to all 173\,262 existing candidate rows, which already have the required ten features computed, at no additional retrieval or labelling cost. This produces two explicitly distinct label tiers, following standard practice in weak and distant supervision: a small \textbf{silver} tier (419 candidates, LLM-judged and human-adjudicated) and a much larger \textbf{bronze} tier (the remaining candidates, model-predicted rather than independently judged). The two tiers are not merged or treated as equally reliable anywhere in this pipeline.

\paragraph{Validating the bronze tier.} To estimate how trustworthy the bronze labels actually are, a random sample of 150 bronze-labelled candidates is independently judged by two more capable models, \texttt{claude-sonnet-5} and \texttt{gpt-5.4} (an upgrade from the \texttt{claude-haiku-4-5}/\texttt{gpt-4o-mini} pair used for the original silver standard, chosen after confirming via the blind calibration check in Section~\ref{subsec:pooled_gold_standard} that the cheaper pair was not itself the limiting factor). Only candidates where both stronger judges unanimously agree are counted, disagreements are excluded from the spot-check rather than guessed at, the same principle used throughout Section~\ref{subsec:pooled_gold_standard}. This validation costs under \$1, compared to the \$22\,000+ a full re-judging pass would require.

\subsection{Expanding the Silver Standard via Active Learning (AL)}
\label{subsec:active_learning}

Rather than judging a random or exhaustive sample of the remaining 96 uncovered queries, the silver standard is expanded using \emph{uncertainty sampling}: the distillation classifier's own predicted probability is used to find, for every one of the 101 queries, the ten candidates it is least confident about (predicted probability closest to 0.5), and only those are sent for judging. This targets exactly the candidates most likely to teach the classifier something it does not already know, rather than spending judging budget on cases it already calls correctly. The judging prompt is enriched at the same time with company metadata that was previously collected but never shown to the judges: \texttt{organization\_type}, \texttt{organization\_size}, \texttt{state}, \texttt{district}, \texttt{municipality}, and \texttt{nace\_code}, alongside the original name, country, and summary fields, giving the LLM judges more grounding for cases a summary alone leaves ambiguous. This active-learning approach is well established in the machine learning literature \citep{settles2009active}, and combining LLM-based and human-provided relevance judgments to build a test collection under a limited labelling budget mirrors the approach of \citet{takehi2024llmassisted}, who propose a similar strategy for balancing manual and LLM annotation effort in IR test collection construction. Uncertainty sampling specifically for building IR test collections, rather than for training a downstream classifier, has direct precedent in \citet{rahman2020efficient}, who show that selecting documents for human judgement by a system's own uncertainty reduces the labelling budget needed to reach a reliable test collection, the same logic applied here to selecting candidates for LLM judgement instead. This is not a universally settled question, however: \citet{althammer2023annotating} find that, for the specific task of selecting training data to fine-tune a neural ranker, active learning strategies did not outperform random selection once labelling cost was accounted for. This thesis's use case differs in an important respect that plausibly explains why uncertainty sampling still pays off here: the goal in Section~\ref{subsec:active_learning} is not to train the best possible ranker directly, but to close a measured, diagnosed coverage gap in the underlying silver standard (Section~\ref{sec:silver_labels_result}), where deliberately targeting the classifier's most uncertain, least-covered queries is exactly the failure mode identified. \citet{xia2025fromselection}'s recent survey of LLM-based active learning situates this thesis's approach within a fast-moving area of the literature, where using an LLM as the labeller within an uncertainty-sampling loop, rather than only as the model being trained, is an increasingly common pattern that this thesis's own active-learning pass follows.

\subsection{Deepening a Headline Query Subset via Round-Based AL}
\label{subsec:headline_deepening}

The shallow, roughly uniform depth left by Section~\ref{subsec:active_learning}'s expansion (10-14 silver-labelled candidates per query) fixes out-of-distribution generalisation but is too thin to report reliable per-query Recall@$k$/NDCG@$k$ as an evaluation benchmark, deeper judged pools reduce the variance of such metrics (Section~\ref{sec:scaling_corpus} discusses this further). Rather than deepening all 101 queries, this thesis selects a set of 14 queries for methodological diversity beyond simple topic variety, covering geographic coverage not tested by the original pilot, the BM25 vocabulary-mismatch failure mode (``venture capital firms''), adjacent-category disambiguation (e.g.\ pharmaceutical/biotech, investment/wealth management), acronym-heavy queries, and niche or emerging industries.

Unlike the one-shot batch in Section~\ref{subsec:active_learning}, this used genuinely iterative active learning: label a round, retrain the classifier on everything labelled so far, re-rank the \emph{remaining} candidates with the \emph{updated} classifier, and repeat, since uncertainty estimates go stale as soon as new labels are added. A small fixed validation set (100 candidates, judged once with the stronger judge pair and reused for free across every round) tracked whether each additional round was still worth its cost.

\paragraph{Refreshing the bronze tier at no additional cost.} The combined 2{,}269-candidate silver standard (1{,}429 from Section~\ref{subsec:active_learning} plus 840 from this section) was used to retrain the distillation classifier from Section~\ref{sec:silver_labels} once more (2{,}267 of the 2{,}269 matched an existing feature row, the same small gap noted in Section~\ref{subsec:pooled_gold_standard}), and applied to the existing candidate pool to produce up to 1{,}000 bronze-labelled candidates per query, capped rather than kept at the full pool size, and with every silver-verified candidate guaranteed a place regardless of its predicted probability rank. This required no new retrieval, encoding, or judging: every one of the 101 queries already has at least 1{,}022 candidates with retrieval features computed (median 1{,}661), so the only new work is classifier inference. All 101 queries now have exactly 1{,}000 candidates each (101{,}000 rows total, 2{,}267 of them silver-verified), the deepest per-query coverage produced so far in this thesis, though still bronze-tier and bounded by the classifier's demonstrated accuracy (Section~\ref{subsec:headline_deepening}) rather than independently verified.

\paragraph{Quantifying how far production's own ranking diverges from the independent silver/bronze set.}
\label{subsec:production_divergence}

\texttt{dataset/production\_results.xlsx}, production's own top-1{,}000-per-query ranking, has exactly the same shape as the refreshed bronze set above (101{,}000 rows, 1{,}000 per query, identical \texttt{query\_id}/\texttt{domain} keys), making a direct, row-level comparison possible with no new judging or encoding cost. Four checks quantify what has, until this point, mostly been argued qualitatively in this thesis: how much production's own output actually diverges from an independently verified standard.

\paragraph{A direct ranking comparison: production vs. an independently cross-validated ranker.}
\label{subsec:goi_vs_ours_ranking}

Section~\ref{subsec:production_divergence} shows production's ranking \emph{differs} from the independent silver/bronze set; this section asks whether it is actually \emph{worse} at finding truly relevant companies, a stronger and more specific claim. Production's own top-1{,}000-per-query ranking is compared against a ranking produced by the fusion-ranker classifier trained with leave-one-query-out cross-validation (the same principle as Section~\ref{subsec:refit_fusion_ranker}, extended from 5 to all 101 queries: for every query, the classifier is trained on every \emph{other} query's silver labels only, so no ranking is ever produced by a model that saw that query's own labels). All 101 folds train in under 15 seconds, so this adds no meaningful cost. Both rankings are scored against the same silver-verified relevant set (\texttt{gold\_label} $=2$) using Recall@$k$/Precision@$k$/NDCG@$k$ at $k \in \{10, 50, 100, 300, 500, 1000\}$.

\subsection[Effect of Judgment Coverage on the Production Comparison]{Does Judgment Coverage, Not Just Ranking Quality, Explain the Production Comparison?}
\label{subsec:label_coverage_bpref}

The explanation offered above, that production's advantage in Section~\ref{subsec:goi_vs_ours_ranking} is a candidate-pool artifact rather than a genuine quality gap, was argued qualitatively. This section makes it concrete and measurable instead, following a specific critique of the evaluation methodology: every precision, recall, and NDCG figure reported in this thesis implicitly treats an unjudged candidate as irrelevant, which punishes a system exactly when it retrieves something good the pooling process happened to miss. If different systems' top-$k$ results carry very different fractions of already-judged candidates, their scores are not measuring the same thing, and are not directly comparable without knowing that fraction.

\paragraph{Method.} For six systems, production's own ranking; the leave-one-query-out cross-validated fusion ranker from Section~\ref{subsec:goi_vs_ours_ranking}; and each of the four individual retrieval channels' own top-1{,}000 ranking within the same candidate pool (BM25, MiniLM, GTE-large, Linq-Embed-Mistral), label coverage is computed as the fraction of a query's top-$k$ results that carry any silver label at all, relevant or not, independently of whether those labels indicate relevance. \texttt{bpref} \citep{buckley2004retrieval}, a metric introduced at TREC specifically for evaluation under incomplete judgments, is computed alongside the usual Recall@1{,}000: rather than treating an unjudged document as confirmed non-relevant, bpref only counts judged non-relevant documents when penalising a relevant document's rank position, so a system is never docked for retrieving something genuinely unjudged rather than genuinely irrelevant.

\subsection[A Production-Independent Test: Companies Production Never Saw]{A Production-Independent Test: Can This Pipeline Find Companies Production Never Saw?}
\label{subsec:production_independent_eval}

Section~\ref{subsec:goi_vs_ours_ranking}'s comparison could not yet support the claim that this thesis's ranking outperforms production, because the silver standard it was scored against was partly pooled from production's own output. This section closes that gap directly, using the random, production-independent 300{,}000-company sample introduced in Section~\ref{sec:scaling_corpus} (\texttt{source == ``new''} in the combined pool, as opposed to \texttt{``existing''} for the original, potentially production-entangled corpus).

\paragraph{Method.} For the same 19 deep-coverage queries used throughout this thesis's rigorous comparisons (a leakage-free held-out set never used for hyperparameter or model selection, Section~\ref{subsec:hyperparameter_tuning}), candidates are pooled from four independent retrieval channels (BM25, MiniLM, GTE-large, Linq-Embed-Mistral), searched exclusively over \texttt{source == ``new''} companies so the pool is never diluted by the already-curated existing corpus, then trust-filtered the same way as the original silver standard (Section~\ref{subsec:pooled_gold_standard}). Every candidate in this pool is, by construction, a company production was never asked to rank for any of these queries, so production's own output cannot have influenced which candidates end up here, this removes the candidate-pool bias identified in Section~\ref{subsec:goi_vs_ours_ranking} by design, not by argument. Trustworthy candidates are then judged by two strong LLM judges from two different labs, Claude Sonnet 5 and Google's Gemini 3.6 Flash, chosen instead of the originally planned GPT-5.4 pairing specifically because this thesis's own OpenAI baseline (\texttt{text-embedding-3-small}, Section~\ref{subsec:openai}) is itself under evaluation, so an OpenAI judge risks the same-family bias this thesis otherwise avoids by construction. Disagreements are resolved by hand, the same tie-breaking principle used for every other silver-standard pass in this thesis.

Because production has no ranking data at all for \texttt{source == ``new''} companies (\texttt{production\_results.xlsx} only ever covers the original corpus), a symmetric production-vs-ours ranking comparison is not even defined here. The meaningful test this section runs instead: do the resulting silver-verified relevant companies, independently and blindly confirmed, get found and ranked well by this thesis's own retrieval pipeline, searching the full, realistic corpus across all four scaling tiers (Section~\ref{sec:scaling_corpus})? This directly tests the coverage-gain claim from Section~\ref{subsec:production_divergence} on a candidate pool that is clean of circularity by construction.

\paragraph{Result.} Of the 1{,}229 trustworthy pooled candidates, both judges produced a usable label for every one; 1{,}130 (91.9\%) agreed unanimously, comparable to the 93.8\% unanimous rate this same upgraded judge pairing reached in Section~\ref{subsec:pooled_gold_standard}. The remaining 99 (8.1\%) sit in the manual tie-break queue and had not yet been resolved at the time of writing; the numbers below use only the 1{,}130 unanimously-agreed labels (label distribution: 227 not relevant, 208 partially relevant, 695 highly relevant), so the confirmed-relevant count of 695 across the 19 queries should be read as a lower bound rather than a final figure, it can only grow once the remaining 99 are resolved.

\paragraph{Is the classifier's confidence honest? A calibration check.}
\label{subsec:calibration_check}

The accuracy figures reported throughout Sections~\ref{sec:silver_labels}--\ref{subsec:headline_deepening} measure whether the classifier's predicted label (relevant/not, at the 0.5 cutoff) is correct. A separate question is whether its predicted \emph{probability} is honest, when it reports 90\% confidence, is it right about 90\% of the time. This matters because the overwhelming majority of the bronze tier was never individually spot-checked; trusting the classifier's confidence at face value depends on it being well calibrated, not just accurate at one threshold. Using the same leave-one-query-out cross-validated predictions as Section~\ref{subsec:goi_vs_ours_ranking}, predicted probabilities for all 2{,}267 silver-labelled candidates were binned into deciles and compared against the actual relevant rate within each bin.

\subsection{Silver-Standard Coverage Across the Ranking}
\label{subsec:external_review_prep}

Before sending ranked candidate lists to external reviewers at Istari (Kerem Cerit and Manav) for a joint relevance-label review, it is worth checking where the silver labels already collected sit within each query's own ranked output, since that determines whether the top of the ranking can already be called trustworthy or whether it is still, in effect, unverified.

\paragraph{Two representative queries.} Query 1 (``software companies'') and query 92 (``wealth management firms'') were chosen to send out, the former for its comparatively large existing label count, the latter for having the largest coverage gap identified in Section~\ref{subsec:production_divergence}.

\paragraph{Method.} For each query, the classifier-ranked top-1{,}000 candidates from Section~\ref{subsec:refit_fusion_ranker}'s bronze-label pipeline were split into five rank bands (1--100, 101--300, 301--500, 501--700, 701--1{,}000), and every candidate already carrying a verified silver label was located within its band.

\paragraph{Implication for the external review.} Two export formats were prepared for Kerem and Manav: the full ranked top-1{,}000 list per query, in rank order, so that rank order alone would front-load the least-verified part of the list (ranks 1--700); and a lighter, curated 100-candidate alternative per query (20 candidates from each of the same five rank bands, prioritising candidates absent from production's own top-1{,}000 for that query, to test directly whether the pipeline surfaces genuinely relevant companies production misses). In both formats, already-labelled candidates remain at their natural position, unflagged, so agreement with this thesis's own silver labels on those rows was intended as a free, blind inter-rater consistency check. Reviewers were given written guidelines matching the standard already used for LLM judging (Section~\ref{subsec:pooled_gold_standard}), also folded directly into the judge prompt from this point forward (Notebook 66) so that human and model judgments apply the same criteria.

\section{Phase IV: Learned Retrieval Revisited}
\label{sec:phase4_method}

\subsection{Hyperparameter Tuning the Fusion Ranker}
\label{subsec:hyperparameter_tuning}

Every fusion ranker result reported so far, the original model (Section~\ref{sec:learned_fusion}), the pooled-silver-standard retraining (Section~\ref{subsec:refit_fusion_ranker}), and the cross-validated ranking comparison against production (Section~\ref{subsec:goi_vs_ours_ranking}), uses the same hand-picked \texttt{HistGradientBoostingClassifier} configuration (\texttt{max\_iter=150}, \texttt{max\_depth=4}, \texttt{class\_weight="balanced"}), chosen once early on and never systematically tuned against the silver standard. This section addresses that gap with a randomized hyperparameter search.

\paragraph{Method.} The search space covers \texttt{max\_depth} (2 to 8, or unlimited), \texttt{max\_iter} (50 to 300), \texttt{learning\_rate} (0.03 to 0.3), \texttt{min\_samples\_leaf} (5 to 30), and \texttt{l2\_regularization} (0.0 to 2.0); \texttt{class\_weight="balanced"} is kept fixed throughout, since it is an established, justified design choice rather than a model-capacity hyperparameter. 40 combinations are sampled at random (fixed seed for reproducibility).

\paragraph{Avoiding leakage in model selection.} Selecting the best combination using the same cross-validated metric later reported as the final result would bias that result upward (the ``winner's curse'' in model selection): a combination is not a fair, unbiased estimate of unseen-data performance if it was chosen \emph{because} it scored best on exactly the data used to report it. To avoid this, the search itself is restricted to the 82 queries without deep silver coverage, evaluated with \texttt{LeaveOneGroupOut} cross-validation grouped by \texttt{query\_id} within that 82-query pool only, scored by mean Recall@1{,}000 and NDCG@1{,}000. The 19 deep-coverage queries (5-query pilot plus 14 headline queries) are never touched during the search. Once the best combination is fixed, it is evaluated fresh with a full \texttt{LeaveOneGroupOut} pass over all 101 queries, the same protocol used for the untuned baseline (Section~\ref{subsec:goi_vs_ours_ranking}). The 19-deep-query subset of that final evaluation is a genuinely leakage-free comparison against the untuned baseline, since those queries never influenced which hyperparameters were chosen; the all-101-query subset is reported alongside it for comparability with earlier sections, but should be read as directional rather than strictly unbiased, since the other 82 queries did inform the search.

\subsection{A Neural Network Fusion Ranker}
\label{subsec:neural_fusion_ranker}

Every fusion-ranker result so far, tuned or not, uses \texttt{HistGradientBoostingClassifier}, a tree ensemble. This section tries a genuinely different model class on the same ten features: a small multi-layer perceptron (MLP), with its own hyperparameter search over architecture (hidden layer sizes and depth), optimization (learning rate), and regularization (dropout, weight decay), asking whether a neural network does any better than the tuned tree ensemble from Section~\ref{subsec:hyperparameter_tuning}.

\paragraph{Formal setup.} As with the GBDT and logistic-regression models (Section~\ref{sec:learned_fusion}), the MLP takes the same ten-dimensional feature vector $x \in \mathbb{R}^{10}$ per (query, candidate) pair as input and is trained against the same pseudo-relevance target $y \in \{0, 1\}$. Each hidden layer applies a linear transformation followed by a ReLU activation, $\mathrm{ReLU}(z) = \max(0, z)$, and dropout at the searched rate; the final layer is a single linear unit producing a raw logit $z$, with no output activation applied directly. The network is trained to minimise a class-weighted binary cross-entropy loss applied to that logit, $\mathcal{L}(y, z) = -\left[w_y \log \sigma(z) \cdot y + \log(1 - \sigma(z)) \cdot (1 - y)\right]$ where $\sigma$ is the sigmoid function and $w_y$ up-weights the minority (relevant) class inversely to its frequency in the training fold, implemented as \texttt{BCEWithLogitsLoss} with a \texttt{pos\_weight} argument in PyTorch, optimised with AdamW.

\paragraph{Method.} Unlike the GBDT search, full \texttt{LeaveOneGroupOut} cross-validation per hyperparameter combination is too expensive here, each fold trains a network from scratch rather than fitting a near-instant tree, so the search phase uses a single, fixed 80/20 split of the 82 tuning-only queries (66 train, 16 validation), never touching the 19 held-out deep queries; this is a standard, well-justified simplification for neural hyperparameter search. 20 combinations are sampled at random over hidden layer sizes ($\{16\}$ to $\{128,64,32\}$), dropout (0 to 0.5), learning rate ($10^{-4}$ to $5\times10^{-3}$), and weight decay (0 to $10^{-2}$), each scored by NDCG@1{,}000 on the 16 validation queries. Two details specific to neural networks that a tree ensemble does not need: features are standardized (zero mean, unit variance, scaler fit on the training split only) before being fed to the network, since gradient descent, unlike a tree split, is sensitive to feature scale; and the loss function is a class-weighted binary cross-entropy (weight inversely proportional to class frequency), mirroring the \texttt{class\_weight="balanced"} convention used by every other classifier in this thesis. Once the best combination is fixed, it is evaluated with the same full \texttt{LeaveOneGroupOut} protocol over all 101 queries used everywhere else, so the comparison against the tuned GBDT on the 19 leakage-free deep queries is direct. Bayesian optimization (mentioned as a candidate search strategy alongside grid and random search) was not implemented, it would require adding \texttt{optuna} as a new dependency, and random search already gave a workable answer at negligible marginal cost given the searches already run in this thesis; noted here as a direction rather than silently dropped.

\subsection{Fine-Tuning the Reranker}
\label{subsec:reranker_finetuning}

Every use of \texttt{BAAI/bge-reranker-v2-m3} throughout this thesis, in the hybrid reranking pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:hybrid_fullpool}) and as one of the ten fusion-ranker features, treats it as a frozen, pretrained component, never fit to this thesis's own queries or labels. This section fine-tunes it directly on the silver-labelled (query, candidate) pairs and checks whether that improves ranking quality, reusing the same 82-tuning/19-held-out query split as Section~\ref{subsec:hyperparameter_tuning} rather than introducing a new one, so the two experiments stay directly comparable and the held-out queries remain untouched by either.

\paragraph{Method.} The reranker is fine-tuned as a regression problem: for each silver-labelled (query, candidate-summary) pair from the 82 tuning-only queries ($\sim$1{,}000 examples), the target score is \texttt{gold\_label}/2 (mapping 0/1/2 to 0.0/0.5/1.0, matching the reranker's own continuous score output), trained with MSE loss, AdamW, learning rate $2\times10^{-5}$, batch size 32, for 4 epochs. This is a small dataset for a $\sim$568M-parameter model, a real overfitting risk flagged explicitly rather than glossed over. Both the original pretrained reranker and the fine-tuned version are then evaluated on the same task the reranker actually performs in this pipeline: reranking each held-out query's full candidate pool from the union-pool feature set (Section~\ref{sec:learned_fusion}, $\sim$1{,}000-2{,}900 candidates per query) and scoring the result by Recall@$k$/NDCG@$k$ against the silver-verified relevant set, restricted to the 19 deep-coverage queries so the comparison is leakage-free by the same logic as Section~\ref{subsec:hyperparameter_tuning}.

\paragraph{A second attempt: hard negatives and a pairwise ranking loss.} Rather than abandoning fine-tuning, the diagnosed cause was addressed directly. Each training query's negative pool was supplemented with candidates sampled from its own retrieval pool that were never silver-labelled at all (a standard, well-justified assumption given how sparse true relevance is in a raw candidate pool), giving 1{,}782 (positive, negative) training pairs across 79 tuning queries, versus only 19 explicit negatives out of 820 individual examples before. Training switched from MSE regression on an absolute score to a pairwise margin ranking loss, directly optimizing for whether a relevant candidate outscores a non-relevant one for the same query, the thing Recall@$k$/NDCG@$k$ actually measure, rather than an absolute score value.

\section{Phase V: Scalability}
\label{sec:phase5_method}

\subsection{Scaling to a Larger Corpus: A Random Sample and Nested Corpus Tiers}
\label{sec:scaling_corpus}

The 98\,716-company corpus used throughout this thesis is not just query-gatekept (Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction}), it is also small relative to Istari's real production dataset. The thesis title's ``at scale'' claim needs a genuine scalability test, does the retrieval pipeline's quality and latency hold up as the pool of companies to search grows substantially larger, not just a claim resting on the existing corpus size.

\paragraph{A fully random sample.} A collaborator at Istari (Kerem Cerit) provided a fully random, query-independent sample of 300\,000 companies drawn from the underlying database, deliberately not filtered or weighted toward any of the 101 queries used elsewhere in this thesis. Randomness here matters methodologically: using this sample to check whether a retrieval system's index or corpus omits genuinely relevant material, rather than to build more query-specific training data, follows the same logic as random-sampling approaches used to estimate search engine index coverage \citep{baryossef2008random}, applied here to check whether the retrieval pipeline still finds known-relevant companies once far more distractor companies are added, rather than to estimate absolute index coverage as in that work.

\paragraph{Data quality.} The sample was profiled and lightly cleaned (data profiling script, not included as a numbered notebook section here for brevity). Of note: only 0.56\% (1\,691 companies) overlapped with the existing 98\,716-company corpus, confirming the sample is genuinely independent rather than a re-draw of existing data. No duplicate domains, no empty summaries, and no literal-string placeholder values were found. State/district/municipality fields were missing 15.7\%/60.4\%/75.4\% of the time respectively, expected sparsity given inconsistent administrative-division reporting across countries, not a data quality defect. A small fraction of rows (1.03\%) shared an exact-duplicate summary with another row, flagged rather than dropped, since a shared summary does not necessarily indicate an invalid company record.

\paragraph{Nested corpus tiers.} Rather than four independently drawn samples of increasing size, the scaling corpus is built as four \emph{nested} tiers: 100\,000, 200\,000, 300\,000, and $\sim$397\,000 (all available) companies, each strictly containing the previous tier plus additional randomly drawn companies from one fixed shuffle. The existing 98\,716-company corpus, which holds all silver-labelled data used for evaluation, is included in every tier without exception, so that a change in a retrieval metric between tiers can be attributed to the added distractor companies, not to silver-labelled companies being dropped from the search pool. This nested design keeps the four tiers directly comparable to one another, following the same logic that motivates careful control of pooling depth in retrieval evaluation more broadly \citep{lu2016effect}.

\paragraph{Encoding.} Every company in the combined pool ($\sim$397\,025 companies) is encoded exactly once with each embedding model, rather than re-encoded separately per tier: an embedding is a fixed, corpus-independent property of a single company's text, so encoding once and subsetting afterward is both correct and avoids redundant computation. BM25 is a deliberate exception, being a corpus-level statistical model rather than a per-document encoding (a term's weight depends on its frequency across the whole indexed collection), its index must be rebuilt separately for each of the four tiers once the scaling evaluation itself is run. The pretrained reranker (\texttt{bge-reranker-v2-m3}) is not part of this encoding step at all, it scores (query, candidate) pairs at retrieval time only, applied to whichever candidates survive first-stage retrieval, regardless of corpus size.

\paragraph{Scope: beyond the three fusion-ranker features.} The scaled encoding was not limited to the three models already inside the trained fusion ranker (MiniLM, GTE-large, Linq-Embed-Mistral). Every embedding model evaluated as a baseline earlier in this thesis (BGE-large, BGE-M3, Nomic, E5-Mistral, SFR-Mistral, SimLM) and ColBERTv2 was also encoded at the new scale. The motivation is the same circularity concern raised in Section~\ref{subsec:refit_fusion_ranker}: the original baseline comparison chapter, including the finding that BGE underperforms MiniLM, was evaluated entirely against the circular \texttt{goi\_search\_results.json} ground truth, before the independent, pooled silver standard existed, and has never been rechecked against it, unlike the fusion ranker's own feature-importance finding, which was rechecked and found to partially reverse (Section~\ref{subsec:refit_fusion_ranker}). Re-scoring every baseline model's already-computed top-1000 rankings against the current non-circular, 1{,}429-candidate silver standard, at zero additional API or GPU cost, is planned as a follow-up to check whether the reported model ranking (MiniLM best, BGE and BGE-M3 below it) survives independent verification the same way the fusion ranker's finding was checked, consistent with the general principle that pooling depth and evaluation-set independence, not just raw retrieval scores, determine how trustworthy a reported ranking of methods actually is \citep{baryossef2008random}.

ColBERT is architecturally distinct from every other model here: it stores one embedding per token rather than one vector per company (`doc\_embs.pt`, shape $[N, 192, 128]$, roughly 19.5\,GB at this scale versus 4.85\,GB for the original 98,716-company run), so it cannot simply be subset into corpus-size tiers the same way a single dense vector per company can; it is included nonetheless, for completeness against every method previously evaluated.

\paragraph{Status.} The combined pool and nested tier membership have been constructed, and encoding is complete: all 10 models (MiniLM, GTE-large, BGE-large, BGE-M3, Nomic, Linq-Embed-Mistral, E5-Mistral, SFR-Mistral, SimLM, ColBERTv2) have produced a full embedding file covering all $\sim$397\,025 companies. The original 98\,716-company Linq-Embed-Mistral run took approximately 34 minutes of GPU time, spread across several resubmitted jobs due to this cluster's per-job wall-time limit and lengthy model-loading time, at roughly 4$\times$ the scale, encoding times for the large AutoModel-based encoders turned out shorter than that estimate once a Hugging Face Hub network-verification hang was diagnosed and fixed, typically well under 30 minutes of pure encoding once loading succeeds.

Two evaluation notebooks answer question (1), does Recall@$k$/NDCG@$k$ and query latency hold up as the corpus grows from roughly 100K to 400K companies, restricted to the 19 queries with the deepest and most reliable silver-standard coverage (the 5-query pilot plus the 14 headline queries from Section~\ref{subsec:active_learning}), so the resulting metrics are not read from an unreliably shallow label set. A per-tier BM25 index notebook builds a separate index for each of the four nested tiers (tokenizing the corpus once and reusing the tokenization across tiers, since only the IDF statistics change with tier size, not the tokens themselves). A scaling-evaluation notebook then covers BM25 alongside the three embedding models already inside the trained fusion ranker (MiniLM, GTE-large, Linq-Embed-Mistral), computing Recall@$k$/NDCG@$k$ against the silver standard and per-query retrieval latency at every tier, for every method (all using exact brute-force search, ANN is treated separately in Section~\ref{subsec:ann_tuning}). Every silver-relevant candidate for the 19 deep queries is present in all four tiers by construction, confirmed directly (906 of 906 relevant candidates found in even the smallest, 100K, tier), so any change in these metrics across tiers is attributable to the growing pool of distractor companies rather than relevant companies dropping out of the search pool.

\subsection{Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN) Index Tuning}
\label{subsec:ann_tuning}

The thesis proposal commits explicitly to approximate nearest neighbor search (``supports scalable top-$k$ retrieval using approximate nearest neighbor search''; ``trade-off between retrieval accuracy and latency under different ANN index configurations''). Section~\ref{sec:ann_scaling} took a first pass at this question early in the thesis, using a synthetic 2-million-vector corpus (real embeddings padded with Gaussian-noise-perturbed filler vectors) and found a 6.5--6.6$\times$ speedup with no meaningful quality loss. Every other result in this thesis, including the scaling evaluation above, still uses exact brute-force search on real data, a full dot product against every company in the tier. This section repeats the ANN question on the real, nested 100K--400K corpus built for the scaling evaluation, rather than a synthetic one, to check whether the earlier finding holds at genuine (if still sub-20-million) scale.

\paragraph{Method.} A FAISS \citep{johnson2017billion} HNSW index is built over GTE-large's embeddings (the strongest all-around performer from the scaling evaluation above) at each of the four corpus tiers. HNSW's hyperparameters are index-structure settings rather than model-training settings: \texttt{M} (graph connections per node) and \texttt{ef\_construction} (build-time search width) are fixed when an index is built; \texttt{ef\_search} (query-time search width) can be swept cheaply against an already-built index without rebuilding. Two build configurations are tried per tier (\texttt{M}=16/\texttt{ef\_construction}=40, a faster/lighter build, and \texttt{M}=32/\texttt{ef\_construction}=200, a slower/higher-quality build), and \texttt{ef\_search} is swept across \{10, 50, 100, 200, 500, 1{,}000\} for each. Unlike Section~\ref{subsec:hyperparameter_tuning}'s single best-combination search, there is no one ``best'' ANN setting, recall and latency trade off against each other by construction, so the natural output is a curve across settings, compared directly against the exact-search numbers in Table~\ref{tab:scaling_eval} for the same model, tiers, and queries.

\section{Phase I: Baselines}
\label{sec:phase1_result}

\subsection{Phase I: Sparse Retrieval Baseline (BM25)}
\label{sec:baseline_sparse_result}
\label{subsec:bm25_qual}

Manual inspection of BM25 results reveals a consistent failure mode arising from the \textit{vocabulary mismatch problem}: BM25 retrieves companies that contain query keywords in their description, regardless of whether those companies operate in the queried domain.

A representative example is the query \textit{``software companies''}. The top BM25 results are predominantly investment banks and venture capital firms whose summaries frequently mention the word \textit{software} in the context of describing their investment portfolio (e.g.\ \textit{``we invest in software, fintech, and SaaS businesses''}). These companies are not software companies: they fund them. BM25 cannot distinguish between a company \textit{operating} in a domain and a company \textit{discussing} that domain. This limitation provides a clear and concrete motivation for semantic retrieval methods that operate on the meaning of text rather than on token overlap.


\subsection{Phase I: Baseline Results}
\label{sec:exp_results}

Recall is the primary metric reported in this thesis, following the priority set by Istari for this task: coverage of relevant companies matters more than precise ranking of the first page of results. Overall, dense retrieval outperforms BM25 at every cut-off, and the strongest single baseline is MiniLM, ahead of both BGE and OpenAI despite being by far the smallest and cheapest model tested. This advantage is most visible at low cut-offs, where semantic matching addresses the vocabulary-mismatch problem that limits BM25 (Section~\ref{subsec:bm25_qual}). Precision@10 improves from 0.838 (BM25) to 0.972 (MiniLM), a 16\% relative gain. This confirms the hypothesis that semantic retrieval better captures the intent of natural-language company search queries, particularly for cases where relevant companies use vocabulary different from the query terms.

At the largest cut-off ($k = 1000$), the dense advantage persists rather than narrowing: BM25 reaches a Recall@1000 of 0.635, compared to MiniLM's 0.741, BGE's 0.726, and OpenAI's 0.728. This is a somewhat different pattern from the BEIR benchmark~\citep{thakur2021beir}, where keyword-based methods often close the gap with dense models at large retrieval depths due to broad lexical matching; here, dense retrieval remains clearly ahead of BM25 at every depth tested.

\paragraph{MiniLM.} Among the three dense models, MiniLM achieves the best result on every metric and every cut-off reported in Table~\ref{tab:main_results}, ahead of both BGE and OpenAI despite having roughly 15 times fewer parameters than BGE and no proprietary training data behind it. NDCG@10 reaches 0.975, Precision@10 reaches 0.972, and Recall@1000 reaches 0.741, the highest of all four methods. The one remaining trade-off is query latency: MiniLM's 80.9\,ms total latency is slower than BGE's 44.4\,ms (Section~\ref{sec:exp_runtime_result}), so a deployment that weights latency heavily might still prefer BGE, but on retrieval quality alone MiniLM is the strongest baseline evaluated in this chapter.

\paragraph{Why does the smallest model win?} This result is worth pausing on, since more parameters and more specialised training usually help, not hurt. A plausible explanation lies in what each model was optimised for. BGE and OpenAI's embedding model are both tuned with hard-negative mining and large-scale passage-retrieval objectives, techniques that pay off most on long, heterogeneous documents where distinguishing a genuine match from a superficially similar distractor is hard. Company summaries in this corpus are short (roughly 60 words on average, Section~\ref{subsec:production_independent_eval_result}) and fairly uniform in structure (name, industry, activity, in a template-like AI-generated style), closer in shape to the short sentence pairs MiniLM was originally fine-tuned on for general sentence similarity. A model's specialised training only helps if that specialisation matches the text it is applied to; here, MiniLM's simpler training objective is a closer match to this corpus's actual text than the larger models' retrieval-specific fine-tuning is. This mirrors, at a smaller scale, the domain-mismatch explanation offered later in this chapter for why SimLM underperforms despite being purpose-pretrained for retrieval (Section~\ref{sec:simlm_result}): the more specialised a model's training, the more that training's own assumptions can work against it on a corpus that does not match them.

\paragraph{BGE vs OpenAI.} BGE and OpenAI remain close to one another across all metrics and cut-offs, though both trail MiniLM. The largest observed difference between them is 0.012 NDCG points (at $k=100$), within the range of query-level variance and not a meaningful quality advantage either way. This finding retains an important practical implication: a free, self-hosted model (BGE) matches a commercial API (OpenAI) on this task, at a fraction of the query latency (Section~\ref{sec:exp_runtime_result}).

\subsection{Corpus Quality: Findings}
\label{sec:trust_filter_result}

\paragraph{How much of the corpus is affected.} This contamination is not spread evenly across query topics. An audit of the pseudo-relevance labels for all 101 queries found that, for the query \textit{``web hosting companies''}, 85.4\% of the 1\,000 ground-truth relevant companies have a boilerplate rather than a company-specific summary; for \textit{``cloud computing providers''} the figure is 73.1\%, and for \textit{``e-commerce platforms''} it is 53.1\%. By contrast, most other query topics in the evaluation set (among them \textit{``software companies''}, \textit{``healthcare providers''}, and \textit{``construction companies''}) show no measurable contamination at all. This pattern is intuitive once the underlying mechanism is understood: boilerplate summaries describe hosting, domain, and e-commerce infrastructure, so they are only likely to be retrieved, and only likely to be judged relevant by the production system's own ranking, for queries whose intent is itself adjacent to that infrastructure. A query about software companies is unlikely to surface a domain registrar's boilerplate text, but a query about web hosting companies is exactly the case in which that boilerplate text looks, on the surface, like a plausible match.

This classifier was validated against 300 companies independently judged by an LLM as having a trustworthy or untrustworthy summary. At its best-performing similarity threshold, the classifier reaches an F1 score of 0.59, with a precision of 0.52 and a recall of 0.69 for detecting untrustworthy summaries.

\paragraph{Why this limitation was not corrected in the reported results.} A precision of 0.52 means that of every two summaries the classifier flags as boilerplate, roughly one is, in fact, a genuine, company-specific summary. Removing or down-weighting every flagged company from the retrieval pipeline would therefore discard a large number of companies that are not actually contaminated, trading one source of error for another rather than removing it. For this reason, no correction for boilerplate contamination was applied to any of the results reported in this thesis, and all recall and precision figures should be read with this limitation in mind, particularly for the small set of query topics identified above as heavily affected. This is reported here as an explicitly known and quantified, but unaddressed, limitation of the underlying corpus rather than as a solved problem: a natural direction for future work is to use the boilerplate-similarity score not as a hard filter but as an additional input feature to a trained model, such as the learned fusion ranker introduced in Section~\ref{sec:learned_fusion}, which is able to learn how much to trust an imperfect signal rather than requiring it to be reliable enough for a fixed decision rule.

\paragraph{Does this get worse at scale?} Applying the same classifier to the nested scaling corpus (Section~\ref{sec:scaling_corpus}) answers this directly rather than leaving it to speculation. The original 98\,716-company corpus is contaminated at a stable 2.84\% at every tier, expected, since it is the same fixed set of companies present in all four. The randomly sampled companies added to reach each larger tier are, however, markedly cleaner, contaminated at only 0.39--0.41\%, roughly a seventh of the original corpus's rate. Because the clean, randomly sampled companies make up a growing share of the total pool at each successive tier, the pool-wide contamination rate actually falls as the corpus scales: 2.81\% at 100K, 1.61\% at 200K, 1.21\% at 300K, and 1.02\% at the full 397K tier. This is a reassuring, if unexpected, result: the boilerplate contamination problem does not compound at scale, it dilutes, most likely because the original 98\,716-company corpus was itself drawn from production's own top-1\,000 results per query (Section~\ref{subsec:dataset}), which concentrates exactly the infrastructure-adjacent queries (Section~\ref{sec:trust_filter}) where boilerplate is most likely to be judged relevant, a bias a uniformly random sample of the broader GOI does not share.

\subsection{Oracle Union Recall Ceiling: Results}
\label{sec:oracle_union_result}

\paragraph{Findings.} The best single channel (MiniLM alone) reaches Recall@1000~=~0.741. The full union of all six channels reaches 0.936 (average combined pool size 1\,949 candidates/query), a gain of +0.196 over MiniLM alone. The best-performing \emph{pair} is MiniLM~+~OpenAI-large, at 0.856 (average pool size 1\,300). A pool-size sweep on this best pair shows the ceiling is highly sensitive to how many candidates are taken per channel: at 100 candidates/channel the union only reaches 0.150 recall, rising to 0.581 at 500, and 0.856 at the full 1\,000.

\paragraph{Interpretation.} Because the six-channel union clears 0.936, well above any individual channel, the different retrieval methods are largely making \emph{complementary}, not correlated, errors: a reranker operating over a modestly sized union pool (rather than a single channel) is likely sufficient to approach this ceiling, and a full fine-tuning pipeline is not obviously necessary to close the remaining gap. The residual 0.064 recall lost even under the full six-channel union is consistent with the hard data-quality ceiling identified in Section~\ref{sec:error_analysis} (duplicated/boilerplate company descriptions that no embedding-based channel can disambiguate).

\section{Phase III: Evaluation Validity}
\label{sec:phase3_result}

\subsection{Building the Silver Standard: Results}
\label{subsec:pooled_gold_standard_result}

\paragraph{Inter-rater reliability.} Percent agreement, reported throughout this thesis so far, overstates reliability on a skewed label distribution: two judges that both default to the majority label (highly relevant) will agree often without adding any real information. Cohen's kappa \citep{cohen1960coefficient} corrects for this by subtracting out the agreement expected by chance, and is standard practice for reporting annotator reliability in computational linguistics and IR test-collection construction \citep{artstein2008inter}. Computed across every judging round run for this thesis (2\,539 doubly-judged candidates in total, Notebook 68), the original judge pair (\texttt{gpt-4o-mini}/Claude Haiku) reaches $\kappa = 0.626$ (82.5\% agreement, $n=2\,280$), and the upgraded pair (\texttt{gpt-5.4}/Claude Sonnet 5) reaches $\kappa = 0.882$ (93.8\% agreement, $n=259$). Using the conventional interpretation scale of \citet{landis1977measurement}, this places the original pair at ``substantial'' agreement (0.61--0.80) and the upgraded pair at ``almost perfect'' agreement (0.81--1.00), giving the earlier decision to upgrade judges for spot-checks and the production-independent evaluation (Section~\ref{subsec:production_independent_eval}) a concrete quantitative justification rather than only the qualitative one offered at the time. The external review from Kerem Cerit and Manav (Section~\ref{subsec:external_review_prep}) has since returned, but LLM-human kappa could not be computed from it: the returned sample overlapped with only 2 of the existing silver-labelled candidates for one query and none for the other, far too few for a meaningful estimate (Section~\ref{subsec:external_review_result}). Only one label column was returned per query rather than one per reviewer, so human-human kappa is not computable from what came back either. Notebook 68 remains written to compute both automatically should a fuller review become available.

\paragraph{Blind calibration against the silver standard.} The kappa figures above measure whether the LLM judges agree with each other, and the small 20-candidate pilot in Section~\ref{subsec:pooled_gold_standard} measures whether the author's blind judgment matches each judge individually. Neither measures whether an independent human agrees with the silver standard as a whole, at a scale large enough to support a proper kappa. To close that gap, the author blind-labelled a separate, much larger sample of 300 candidates stratified across 15 queries, five industry-specific, five geographic, five functional, drawn only from queries with deep existing silver coverage, and compared the result against the existing silver labels (Notebook 72). Labels were assigned with no rank or prior label visible, using the same guidelines given to the LLM judges. Raw agreement was 78.7\% (236/300). Cohen's kappa reached 0.603 on the original 3-class scheme and 0.415 once collapsed to a binary relevant/not-relevant split, ``substantial'' and ``moderate'' respectively on the interpretation scale of \citet{landis1977measurement}. Disagreements were not one-sided: of the 64 disagreeing candidates, 33 had the author judging more strictly than the silver label and 31 more loosely, showing no systematic bias in either direction. Agreement varied considerably by query. Four queries fell at or below 60\% exact agreement, and three of them, all geographic (``tech companies in Munich'', ``manufacturing in Baden-W\"urttemberg'', ``logistics companies in the US''), also had kappa at or below 0.25. The fourth, ``managed service providers (MSPs)'', reached a more moderate kappa of 0.38 despite its low raw agreement. The remaining eleven queries ranged from 75\% to a perfect 100\% agreement. This pattern points to genuinely harder judgement calls on geographic queries specifically, where relevance can hinge on a company's registered location versus where it actually operates, rather than to a general problem with the silver standard.

\subsection{Retraining Against the New Silver Standard: Results}
\label{subsec:refit_fusion_ranker_result}

From this point onward, this thesis reports Recall@$k$ and NDCG@$k$ but not Precision@$k$. The silver standard is pooled rather than exhaustive, so an unlabelled candidate cannot be scored as a false positive even when it is genuinely relevant. Recall@$k$ and NDCG@$k$ are computed only over candidates that already carry a label, so this gap does not bias them. Precision@$k$ would be biased, since some of a method's unlabelled retrieved candidates are almost certainly relevant and would inflate the apparent false-positive count if simply treated as irrelevant. Section~\ref{subsec:honest_synthesis} discusses this limitation, and its remaining effect on Recall and NDCG, in full.

\paragraph{Finding.} Retraining on the pooled, independently judged silver standard modestly improves ranking quality over simply reusing the original model's scores (NDCG@5: 0.915~$\to$~0.934; NDCG@10: 0.930~$\to$~0.952), confirming that the new labels carry a genuine, learnable signal rather than just noise. More importantly, the original interpretive finding from Section~\ref{sec:learned_fusion} does not replicate: \texttt{invrank\_minilm}, previously the dominant feature by a wide margin ($+5.014$, more than double the next-largest coefficient), collapses not merely toward zero but to a small \emph{negative} coefficient ($-0.909$). The most plausible explanation is that MiniLM's ranking correlated strongly with production's own circular output specifically, rather than with relevance itself, a correlation that a model trained and evaluated against that same circular output could not distinguish from a genuine relevance signal. \texttt{score\_linq} (Linq-Embed-Mistral's raw similarity score) is the new dominant feature ($+1.965$), and \texttt{invrank\_bm25} is now strongly negative ($-1.252$): being ranked highly by BM25 is associated with \emph{lower} true relevance in this sample. This corroborates, independently and statistically, the qualitative BM25 failure mode already documented in Section~\ref{subsec:bm25_qual}, where investment firms and venture capital companies are ranked highly by BM25 for the query ``software companies'' because they mention the word in passing while describing their investment portfolio, without themselves being software companies.

\paragraph{Confirmation at full scale.} The Limitations paragraph below originally flagged this as a 5-query pilot whose specific coefficients should be read cautiously. Section~\ref{subsec:active_learning}'s expanded silver standard (1{,}429 candidates across all 101 queries) makes it possible to check this directly: retraining the same architecture on the full 101-query silver standard (rightmost column of Table~\ref{tab:pooled_weights}) does not weaken the finding, if anything it sharpens it. \texttt{invrank\_minilm} remains negative ($-1.095$, was $-0.909$ at 5 queries), and \texttt{invrank\_bm25} becomes even more strongly negative ($-2.246$, was $-1.252$), now the single largest-magnitude coefficient in the model. \texttt{score\_linq} remains the dominant positive feature throughout all three columns. The BM25 vocabulary-mismatch failure mode is therefore not an artefact of the ``software companies'' pilot query specifically, it holds consistently once every one of the 101 query topics contributes labelled examples.

\paragraph{Limitations.} This result covers 5 of 101 queries and 417 candidates, a genuine pilot rather than a full replication, and the specific numerical coefficients should be read with that scale in mind. However, the magnitude of the reversal on what was previously the single dominant feature is unlikely to be an artefact of sample size alone, and is treated here as a preliminary but suggestive finding: the interpretive claims drawn from a model trained on circular pseudo-relevance labels should not be taken as descriptions of genuine relevance without independent validation. Scaling this pooled, multi-judge silver standard to the full 101-query set is a direction for future work.

\subsection{Extending the Independent Evaluation: Bronze Labels and Active Learning}
\label{sec:silver_labels_result}

\paragraph{Status.} The bronze-labelling classifier has been trained and applied to all 173\,262 candidates. Of the 150 spot-checked bronze candidates, 132 (88\%) received unanimous agreement between \texttt{gpt-5.4} and \texttt{claude-sonnet-5}; against that unanimous subset, the bronze labels achieved 65.9\% accuracy (precision on the relevant class 65.3\%, recall 85.3\%). A naive baseline that always predicts the majority class on this same 132-candidate sample would score 56.8\%, so the bronze labels carry a real, learnable signal, but a modest one: the classifier over-predicts relevance (34 false positives against 11 false negatives out of 132), consistent with the 75.4\% overall relevant-rate it assigns across the full bronze tier.

The most likely explanation for the gap between this figure and the substantially higher NDCG improvement reported in Section~\ref{subsec:refit_fusion_ranker} is a training-distribution mismatch rather than a flaw in the distillation approach itself: only 8 of the 132 unanimously-judged spot-check candidates (6\%) fall under one of the 5 queries the underlying silver standard was built from, the remaining 124 (94\%) come from query topics the classifier never saw a single labelled example of during training. Generalising from 5 narrow query topics to the other 96 is a substantially harder test than the leave-one-query-out cross-validation in Section~\ref{subsec:refit_fusion_ranker}, which only ever held out one of those same 5 topics at a time. This points toward a concrete, affordable fix rather than abandoning the approach: expanding the silver standard to a larger, more topically diverse subset of the 101 queries (at the roughly \$22\,000 cost estimated above for full-depth pooling) before the resulting bronze labels are relied on broadly, rather than distilling from 5 queries alone.

\subsection{Active Learning: Results}
\label{subsec:active_learning_result}

\paragraph{Result.} This active-learning pass covers all 101 queries (versus the original 5) and adds 1{,}010 newly judged candidates, 852 (84.4\%) agreed unanimously between the two judges, 158 needed human tie-breaking, a disagreement rate close to the original pilot's 16\% once an initial data-merging bug that had corrupted roughly 40\% of the first labelling pass with a missing query field was found and fixed. Combined with the original 419, the expanded silver standard now contains \textbf{1{,}429 candidates spanning all 101 queries} (label distribution: 63 not relevant, 381 partially relevant, 985 highly relevant), a nearly 3.5$\times$ increase in size and full query coverage instead of a 5\% sample of it.

\paragraph{Result.} The distillation classifier was retrained on 1{,}427 of the 1{,}429 silver-labelled candidates spanning all 101 queries (two lacked a matching feature row, the same small gap noted for the pilot in Section~\ref{subsec:pooled_gold_standard}) and re-applied to all 173\,262 candidates. A fresh 150-candidate spot-check, judged with the same stronger judge pair, found bronze-label accuracy rose from \textbf{65.9\% to 80.7\%}; since the majority-class baseline also shifted (56.8\%~$\to$~64.3\%), the more informative figure is the margin over it, which nearly doubled (9.1 to 16.4 percentage points). Most directly confirming the original diagnosis: 100\% of the unanimously-judged spot-check candidates this time fell under a query the classifier had been trained on, compared to only 6\% previously. The out-of-distribution generalisation gap identified in the first spot-check was therefore a direct consequence of training on 5 of 101 query topics, not a flaw in the distillation approach itself, and expanding query coverage resolved it.

This does not reverse Section~\ref{subsec:goi_vs_ours_ranking}'s finding into an outright win, production still edges out the cross-validated ranker under bpref too, but it substantially narrows the gap and confirms quantitatively, not only qualitatively, that part of the original comparison's apparent production advantage was a coverage artifact of the pooling process rather than a genuine difference in ranking quality. \paragraph{Qualitative error analysis: what does the pipeline still miss at 400K?} Section~\ref{sec:error_analysis} examined MiniLM's early misses against the original pseudo-relevance protocol; the same question is worth asking of this thesis's best available result too, not only its strongest single-model baseline. Re-encoding the 19 queries with MiniLM and GTE-large and re-ranking the full 400K tier identifies 77 companies (out of 695) that both methods miss from their own top-1{,}000, despite being independently confirmed highly relevant. A recurring pattern in these misses, read across queries, is signal dilution rather than outright irrelevance: the company genuinely matches the query, but the matching capability is a minor part of a longer, differently-themed description rather than its dominant theme. For the query ``software companies'', several missed companies are unambiguously software vendors, Dataflex Software GmbH (glass-industry software), Waterloo Manufacturing Software (production scheduling software), Dolphin Software (microfinance software), but each summary foregrounds its specific industry vertical rather than the word ``software'' or a generic software-company framing, so the dominant semantic content of the description is closer to ``glass industry'' or ``microfinance'' than to ``software company'' as a category, even though it is one. A related pattern appears for ``companies providing cloud migration services'': PROMAIN offers cloud migration among six or seven other listed services (business process analysis, IT consulting, data analytics, AI/ML, low-code development), so ``cloud migration'' is present but not the description's dominant theme. This is not primarily a description-length effect, missed companies' summaries average 60 words, in line with the corpus generally, it is specifically about which theme a company's own description foregrounds. This complements rather than duplicates the vocabulary-mismatch failure mode already documented for BM25 (Section~\ref{subsec:bm25_qual}, where a company mentions a query term without being a match): here, dense embedding models can under-rank a company that genuinely is a match, when its own description emphasises a narrower or different framing than the query's own vocabulary.

\paragraph{What this answers, and what it still does not.} This is the strongest evidence in this thesis that the retrieval pipeline finds and ranks genuinely production-blind relevant companies well, and at meaningfully large scale. It is not, strictly, the head-to-head ``does this pipeline outperform production'' comparison Section~\ref{subsec:goi_vs_ours_ranking} set out to make: production has no ranking data at all for these companies, so a symmetric comparison remains undefined by construction, not just difficult, as already stated in the Method above. Read together, the two results are complementary rather than contradictory: Section~\ref{subsec:goi_vs_ours_ranking} showed the one existing head-to-head test was compromised by pooling bias in production's favour; this section shows that once that bias is removed by using a genuinely blind candidate pool, the pipeline finds the large majority of what is actually there. That is the central open question this thesis has been explicit about not having closed (Section~\ref{subsec:honest_synthesis}), substantially, if not completely, addressed.

\subsection{Synthesis of Evaluation Findings}
\label{subsec:honest_synthesis}

\paragraph{Part 1: is the silver/bronze standard better than GOI's own output?} Pulling together every check in Sections~\ref{sec:silver_labels}--\ref{subsec:calibration_check}, the answer is not a single yes or no, it depends on which specific claim is being made, and the evidence does not point the same way for each:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Is the labelling methodology more defensible than trusting production's own output as ground truth?} Yes. Production's ranking cannot validate itself; it is circular by construction (Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction}). This thesis's silver standard uses independent multi-judge LLM labelling, blind calibration (Section~\ref{subsec:pooled_gold_standard}), and human tie-breaking, none of which production's own ranking has any equivalent of.
\item \textbf{Does this thesis's methodology find relevant companies production's own system misses entirely?} Yes, demonstrated: 27.3\% of silver-verified highly relevant companies are entirely absent from production's top-1{,}000 (Section~\ref{subsec:production_divergence}, Check 3).
\item \textbf{Does this thesis's ranking algorithm outperform production's ranking at retrieving relevant companies, head-to-head?} Still not directly shown, a symmetric head-to-head comparison is undefined by construction for the one candidate pool production never saw (Section~\ref{subsec:production_independent_eval}), and the one direct head-to-head test that was possible (Section~\ref{subsec:goi_vs_ours_ranking}) found the opposite, likely attributable to residual candidate-pool bias rather than a genuine quality reversal. The strongest available substitute evidence now exists, though: on a candidate pool production had no part in constructing, the pipeline recovers 88.5\% of independently, blindly verified relevant companies at full scale (Section~\ref{subsec:production_independent_eval}), which is complementary evidence for the same underlying claim rather than the strict head-to-head test itself.
\item \textbf{Is the classifier itself highly accurate and well-calibrated?} Only moderately. Brier score 0.220, with a real, systematic calibration gap in the low-to-middle confidence range (Section~\ref{subsec:calibration_check}).
\end{itemize}

The defensible claim this thesis supports is therefore narrower than ``the new labels are better'': \emph{the independent silver standard is methodologically sounder for evaluation purposes and demonstrates concrete, measurable blind spots in production's own ranking, but, as Section~\ref{subsec:goi_vs_ours_ranking} found, does not yet establish that this thesis's own ranking outperforms production's.} Confirming or refuting that stronger claim requires evaluating against a silver-verified relevant set drawn from candidates production had no part in surfacing. The random, production-independent sample and nested scaling corpus described next (Section~\ref{sec:scaling_corpus}) were built with exactly this in mind, and the resulting production-independent test (Section~\ref{subsec:production_independent_eval}) now substantially, if not completely, addresses it.

\paragraph{Part 2: once the silver standard existed, did tuning and fine-tuning the pipeline against it actually help?} Sections~\ref{subsec:hyperparameter_tuning}--\ref{subsec:ann_tuning} ran four further, genuinely independent experiments against that silver standard, each with its own leakage-free held-out comparison, and the results are again mixed rather than uniformly positive. Wherever a numerical gap between two methods is reported below, it is checked with a \emph{paired significance test} (the Wilcoxon signed-rank test), a widely recommended choice for exactly this kind of per-query comparison in IR evaluation \citep{smucker2007comparison}: rather than just comparing two mean scores, it compares the two methods query-by-query on the same 19 queries and asks whether one method winning more often than the other could plausibly be due to chance alone. A small $p$-value means the gap is unlikely to be noise; a large one means the sample of 19 queries is too small to tell the two methods apart with confidence, even if their point estimates differ:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Does systematically tuning the fusion ranker's hyperparameters help?} Inconclusively. Recall@1{,}000 and NDCG@1{,}000 move up while NDCG@10 moves down, a directionally plausible broad-list-versus-top-of-list trade-off, but a paired significance test on the 19 leakage-free queries finds none of these three differences statistically distinguishable from noise ($p=0.06$--$0.18$, Notebook 71), so this should be read as suggestive rather than established (Section~\ref{subsec:hyperparameter_tuning}).
\item \textbf{Does a different model class, a small neural network, do better than the tuned tree ensemble?} At broad cutoffs, yes, and this is the one statistically confirmed improvement in this thesis: the neural network's NDCG@1{,}000 gain over both the untuned and tuned gradient-boosted tree ensemble is significant under a paired test ($p=0.003$ in both cases, Notebook 71). Its apparent top-of-list gain (NDCG@10) points the same direction but is not statistically distinguishable from noise at $n=19$ (Section~\ref{subsec:neural_fusion_ranker}), and the result is based on a single random architecture search rather than the fully cross-validated search used for the tree ensemble, so it is the most defensible positive result of any improvement attempted here, not an unqualified one.
\item \textbf{Does fine-tuning the pretrained reranker on this task's own labels help?} It depends entirely on how the training signal is constructed. A first attempt, trained on severely imbalanced data (2.3\% negative examples), made the reranker worse at every cutoff. A second attempt, with explicit hard negatives and a pairwise ranking loss, reversed this and genuinely beat the pretrained reranker at $k=500$ and $k=1{,}000$, while still trailing it at $k=10$ and $k=50$ (Section~\ref{subsec:reranker_finetuning}), the same top-of-list-versus-broad-list trade-off seen in the hyperparameter tuning result.
\item \textbf{Does approximate search remain viable once the corpus is scaled up?} Yes, clearly, and this is the one experiment in this group without a trade-off: HNSW-based ANN search matches exact search's Recall@1{,}000/NDCG@1{,}000 within 0.5--2.5\% while running 25--78$\times$ faster across every corpus tier tested, and at the 400K tier this near-equivalence is statistically confirmed, not just numerically small (paired Wilcoxon $p=0.86$--$0.92$, Notebook 71), confirming at real (if still sub-20-million) scale the same favourable trade-off first observed on a synthetic corpus earlier in this thesis (Section~\ref{subsec:ann_tuning}).
\end{itemize}

A pattern recurs across three of these four experiments: whenever a model's training or search objective is reweighted toward broad ranking quality (a large Recall@$k$/NDCG@$k$ at high $k$), it tends to gain coverage at some cost to precision at the very top of the list, and neither direction is unconditionally better, the right choice depends on whether the pipeline's downstream consumer looks at a handful of results directly or takes a broad candidate pool for further processing (in this pipeline's own case, the latter, Section~\ref{sec:hybrid_fullpool}). The neural network result is the one clear exception to that pattern, and the ANN result is not subject to it at all, approximate search's speed gain here costs essentially no quality anywhere in the ranking. Taken together with Part 1, this thesis's honest position is that its methodology is demonstrably more rigorous than trusting production's own output, that several concrete, measurable improvements to the pipeline itself have been found (a neural fusion ranker, a properly-trained fine-tuned reranker, and a large, safe efficiency gain from ANN search), and that the single strongest possible claim, that the resulting system retrieves relevant companies more effectively than production overall, remains open pending the production-independent evaluation described in the next section.

\paragraph{Limitation: precision is a lower bound, not an exact figure.} Every precision@$k$ number reported in this thesis is computed against the pooled, actively-sampled silver standard, not an exhaustive judgment of every retrieved candidate. A retrieved company only counts as a true positive if it happens to have already been silver-labelled; a genuinely relevant company that was simply never pooled or judged contributes neither a true positive nor a false positive; it is silently invisible to the metric. Recall@$k$ is unaffected by this, since it is computed only over the set of candidates that do have a silver label. Precision@$k$, however, is systematically biased downward by an unknown amount, since some fraction of a method's retrieved-but-unlabelled candidates are almost certainly relevant and simply were never reviewed. This is a known consequence of evaluating against pooled rather than exhaustive judgments \citep{baryossef2008random, lu2016effect}, not a flaw specific to this thesis's pipeline, but it means precision figures throughout should be read as conservative lower bounds rather than exact values.

\paragraph{A second, distinct bias: active-learning sampling skews which relevant companies get verified, not just how many.} The previous paragraph's claim that Recall@$k$ is unaffected by incomplete pooling is correct in a narrow, technical sense, that metric is self-consistent with respect to whichever set of candidates carries a label, but it does not follow that Recall@$k$ is an unbiased estimate of recall against the true, natural population of relevant companies. Section~\ref{subsec:active_learning}'s active-learning step selects candidates for labelling by \emph{uncertainty sampling}: for every query, the ten candidates the distillation classifier is currently least confident about (predicted probability closest to 0.5) are the ones sent for judging, exactly the candidates hardest to classify, by construction. This is a well-established and reasonable strategy for improving a classifier efficiently under a limited labelling budget \citep{settles2009active}, and was the only realistic way to extend silver-standard coverage from 5 to all 101 queries within this thesis's timeframe; the alternative, randomly or exhaustively judging enough candidates per query for full coverage without any targeting, risked approaching the \$28\,000 full-judging figure estimated in Section~\ref{sec:silver_labels} rather than the far smaller cost this active-learning pass actually incurred by judging only ten uncertain candidates per query. But it also means the resulting silver-verified relevant set is not a random or representative sample of all relevant companies, it is disproportionately made up of boundary, ambiguous cases that were hardest to classify in the first place. If genuinely relevant companies with thin or ambiguous descriptions are, in general, also harder for any retrieval method to rank highly (a plausible mechanism, since the same weak signal that confuses the classifier would also weaken a dense embedding's similarity score or a keyword match), then Recall@$k$ measured against this actively-sampled set likely understates the recall these methods would achieve against a random, representative sample of relevant companies. Both Recall@$k$ and Precision@$k$ throughout this thesis should therefore be read as conservative estimates for two distinct reasons rather than one: incomplete pooling depth (previous paragraph) and active-learning sampling bias (this paragraph). Disentangling exactly how much each contributes would require judging a genuinely random sample of the full candidate pool at additional labelling cost, and is left as future work alongside the exhaustive-ranking extension described next.

\paragraph{A third bias, harder to quantify: the author personally breaks judge disagreements, then those decisions score the author's own system.} Throughout this thesis, whenever the two LLM judges disagree on a candidate's relevance (roughly 16\% of candidates across the rounds reported in Section~\ref{sec:silver_labels}), the disagreement is resolved by manual review, and that manual review was done by the author. Those resolved labels then feed directly into the silver standard used to score every retrieval method compared in this thesis, including the ones the author designed and tuned. This is a real conflict of interest that cannot be removed retroactively, only disclosed and partially mitigated. Two things were done to limit it, though neither fully closes the gap. First, the blind calibration check reported in Section~\ref{subsec:pooled_gold_standard_result} showed the author's own blind judgments (made without seeing the existing silver label) matched it 78.7\% of the time ($\kappa = 0.603$), evidence the author is not wildly out of step with the judges in isolation, but that check tests general labelling agreement, not tie-breaking objectivity specifically, and does not rule out the more subtle failure mode of resolving genuinely ambiguous cases in whichever direction happens to favour a result the author expects or hopes to see. Second, and more directly, the joint external review with Istari's Kerem Cerit and Manav (Section~\ref{subsec:external_review_prep}) provides a check that does not run through the author at all; in practice, the returned sample turned out too small and too disjoint from the existing silver-labelled candidates to yield the human-human and LLM-human kappa figures originally intended (Section~\ref{subsec:external_review_result}), so this specific bias is not yet closed by an independent quantitative check. A cleaner fix for future work would be blind tie-breaking: routing disagreement cases to a reviewer, ideally not the author, without revealing which system's ranking surfaced the candidate or at what position, so the resolution genuinely cannot be influenced by which result it will end up scoring. That safeguard was not implemented here, and its absence should be read as a limitation of the tie-breaking process itself, not just of who happened to perform it.

\paragraph{Future work: tightening this bound with more exhaustive LLM ranking.} A more exhaustive per-query relevance pass, an LLM directly ranking or scoring every candidate in an already-pooled, multi-source candidate set (rather than the smaller, actively-sampled subset actually judged in this thesis), would shrink the gap between reported and true precision by leaving fewer retrieved candidates unjudged. This must still draw its candidates from a pooled, multi-source set rather than a single retrieval method's own output, otherwise the same single-source circularity this thesis's silver standard was built to avoid (Section~\ref{subsec:pooled_gold_standard}) reappears one layer downstream, with the LLM only ever able to rank whatever one method chose to surface. Scaling this idea further, toward Istari's full company database rather than the $\sim$98\,716-\,to-397\,025-company samples used throughout this thesis, is a natural direction: the same pool-then-judge structure would need to repeat at that scale, with the resulting broader, automated relevance judgments used both as a larger silver standard and as the basis for the production top-1{,}000 retrieval this thesis's methods ultimately aim to support. Neither extension is implemented here; both are left as concrete next steps.

\subsection{What the External Review Found}
\label{subsec:external_review_result}

The curated 100-candidate export was the one returned for both queries, not the full top-1{,}000, so the results below reflect that lighter, non-production-prioritised sample rather than a full-ranking review.

\paragraph{The free consistency check had too little overlap to compute.} Only 2 of the 100 returned candidates for query 1 already carried an internal silver label (1 agreement, 1 disagreement), and none of the 100 for query 92 did, both far too few for a meaningful Cohen's kappa. Of the 189 returned candidates not present in production's results for their query (100 of 100 for query 1, 89 of 100 for query 92), \textbf{100\% were judged at least partially relevant, and 171 (90.5\%) highly relevant}, by a human reviewer with no visibility into the pipeline's own confidence or ranking. This is consistent with, and reinforces, the general lesson from Section~\ref{subsec:hyperparameter_tuning}: what a model is trained or tuned against determines what it gets good at, and a training signal this skewed was never likely to preserve, let alone improve, ranking quality.

\paragraph{Finding.} At a moderate \texttt{ef\_search} setting (500), ANN retrieval matches exact search within 0.5--2.5\% on both Recall@1{,}000 and NDCG@1{,}000 at every tier, while running 25--78$\times$ faster (Table~\ref{tab:ann_tuning}). A paired significance test at the 400K tier (Wilcoxon signed-rank over the same 19 queries, Notebook 71) supports this reading directly rather than only by the size of the point-estimate gap: neither the Recall@1{,}000 difference ($p=0.918$) nor the NDCG@1{,}000 difference ($p=0.860$) between exact and ANN search is remotely close to significant, with 95\% paired-bootstrap confidence intervals straddling zero in both cases ($[-0.025, +0.018]$ and $[-0.013, +0.007]$ respectively). This is not a free lunch at every setting, the trade-off the proposal asks to characterize is real at the cheap end: at \texttt{ef\_search}=10, recall collapses to 0.16--0.28 depending on tier and build configuration, far below exact search, confirming that ANN's speed advantage is bought at real cost to recall once search width is set too aggressively. The cheaper build configuration is therefore the more practical choice overall: fast to build, fast to query, and recall/NDCG effectively indistinguishable from exact search once \texttt{ef\_search} is set to 500 or above.

This experiment's 25--78$\times$ speedup is substantially larger than Section~\ref{sec:ann_scaling}'s earlier 6.5--6.6$\times$ finding on the synthetic 2-million-vector corpus; the two are not a strictly controlled comparison, since they use different embedding models, different exact-search implementations, and different corpora, so the specific magnitudes should not be compared directly. What matters is that both experiments, one on synthetic filler data early in this thesis, one on the real, nested scaling corpus built later, agree on the same qualitative conclusion: approximate search is a large, low-risk efficiency gain for this retrieval task, with negligible quality cost at a well-chosen operating point.

\section{Overall Experimental Findings}
\label{sec:overall_findings}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

This chapter has moved through five phases: establishing baselines, improving retrieval under the original evaluation protocol, discovering and addressing that protocol's circularity, revisiting the learned pipeline against an independent standard, and testing the result at larger scale. This section draws those threads together compressively rather than re-arguing them; the evidence for each claim below is developed in full in the sections cited.

\subsection{Retrieval Performance}
\label{subsec:overall_retrieval}

Under the production-derived pseudo-relevance protocol, dense retrieval substantially outperformed the lexical BM25 baseline: the strongest single dense model, MiniLM, reached Recall@1{,}000~$=$~0.741 against BM25's 0.635, ahead of both BGE (0.726) and OpenAI (0.728) despite being by far the smallest and cheapest model tested (Section~\ref{sec:exp_results}). Not every subsequent extension improved on this: query expansion via HyDE reduced recall and added substantial latency (Section~\ref{sec:hyde}), and swapping the pretrained reranker for an alternative model changed Recall@1{,}000 by only 0.0007 (Section~\ref{sec:reranker_ablation}), showing that the specific reranker model, as distinct from whether a model is trained on this task's own data at all, was not the limiting factor. Combining complementary retrieval signals did help: a three-channel candidate union reranked with a pretrained cross-encoder reached Recall@1{,}000~$=$~0.804 (Section~\ref{sec:hybrid_fullpool}), and training a fusion model directly on this task's own queries closed most of the remaining gap to the six-channel oracle ceiling of 0.936 (Section~\ref{sec:oracle_union}), reaching Recall@1{,}000~$=$~0.823, the strongest result obtained under this protocol (Section~\ref{sec:learned_fusion}).

\subsection{Evaluation Validity}
\label{subsec:overall_validity}

The strongest methodological finding in this chapter is not that one retrieval model outperforms another, but that the evaluation protocol behind every result in the previous section is circular: it treats production's own output as ground truth, so a system cannot be shown to beat production if production defines what counts as correct (Section~\ref{subsec:pooled_gold_standard}). Building and expanding an independently judged silver standard (Sections~\ref{subsec:pooled_gold_standard}, \ref{subsec:active_learning}) made it possible to quantify what that circularity hides rather than only argue that it exists: production's own ranking misses 27.3\% of independently verified highly relevant companies outright, and its per-query ranking carries almost no relationship to independently verified relevance (mean Spearman $-0.056$, Section~\ref{subsec:production_divergence}). The circularity is not only a labelling concern, either: retraining the learned fusion ranker's own interpretive model on the new standard reverses one of its most prominent findings, MiniLM's inverse rank goes from the dominant positive feature ($+5.014$) under the original protocol to a small negative one ($-1.095$) under the independent standard (Section~\ref{subsec:refit_fusion_ranker}), evidence that a circular evaluation protocol can produce an apparently meaningful model interpretation that does not survive independent verification. The full synthesis of this investigation, including its own limitations, is given in Section~\ref{subsec:honest_synthesis}.

\subsection{Independent Evaluation of the Learned Pipeline}
\label{subsec:overall_independent}

Quantifying production's blind spots does not, by itself, show that this thesis's own pipeline retrieves relevant companies more effectively than production does: the most direct test available found production's ranking ahead at every cutoff, an artefact of production being one of the six sources pooled into the silver standard's candidate set rather than a genuine quality reversal (Section~\ref{subsec:goi_vs_ours_ranking}). A production-independent test, restricted entirely to companies production had no opportunity to already rank, avoids this bias by construction: on that candidate pool, Linq-Embed-Mistral recovers 88.5\% of independently, blindly verified relevant companies at the largest tested corpus tier (Section~\ref{subsec:production_independent_eval}), the strongest available evidence that the coverage gains claimed throughout this chapter are genuine rather than a pooling artefact, short of a symmetric head-to-head comparison that is not definable for a pool production never ranked at all. Re-tuning the learned pipeline against the independent standard gave genuine, if uneven, gains: hyperparameter tuning improved broad-list metrics without reaching statistical significance (Section~\ref{subsec:hyperparameter_tuning}), a small neural network fusion ranker's broad-list improvement over the tuned tree ensemble was statistically confirmed ($p=0.003$, Section~\ref{subsec:neural_fusion_ranker}), and a properly reformulated reranker fine-tuning attempt beat its pretrained starting point at deeper cutoffs, after an initial, uninformative attempt had failed outright (Section~\ref{subsec:reranker_finetuning}).

\subsection{Scalability and Efficiency}
\label{subsec:overall_scalability}

The pipeline was tested at a nested corpus scale of up to 397,025 companies, roughly 2\% of Istari's full 20-million-company database (Section~\ref{sec:scaling_corpus}); this establishes a scalability trend up to the tested size rather than end-to-end performance at full production scale. Across that range, recall degraded gradually rather than catastrophically as distractor companies were added, and approximate nearest-neighbour search matched exact search's Recall@1{,}000 and NDCG@1{,}000 within 0.5--2.5\% while running 25--78$\times$ faster, a difference a paired significance test confirmed was not distinguishable from noise (Section~\ref{subsec:ann_tuning}). This result held qualitatively on an earlier, synthetic 2-million-vector test as well (Section~\ref{sec:ann_scaling}), giving two independent pieces of evidence for the same conclusion: approximate search is a large, low-risk efficiency gain for this retrieval task.

\chapter{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{ch:conclusion}

\section{Summary of Contributions}

This thesis set out to answer a specific question (Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction}): can embedding-based semantic retrieval, combined with approximate nearest-neighbour search, generate accurate top-$k$ candidate sets from a large company dataset while keeping query latency low, and how does it compare to Istari's own production system? The evidence gathered across this thesis answers each part of that question directly rather than in the abstract.

On retrieval quality, a systematic comparison of more than ten dense embedding models, a late-interaction architecture, and several hybrid pipelines converged on a learned fusion ranker, trained on this task's own queries rather than assembled from fixed, hand-designed rules, as the strongest method evaluated against the original pseudo-relevance protocol (Recall@1{,}000~$=$~0.823, Section~\ref{sec:learned_fusion}). Because no human relevance judgements exist for this task, that result alone would rest entirely on agreement with production's own, potentially circular, output. This thesis therefore also built an independent, multi-judge, blind-calibrated silver standard from scratch (Section~\ref{subsec:pooled_gold_standard}), expanded it via active learning to cover all 101 queries (Section~\ref{subsec:active_learning}), and used it to re-examine the fusion ranker's own interpretive claims, revealing that its most prominent feature-importance finding did not survive independent validation (Section~\ref{subsec:refit_fusion_ranker_result}) and that production's own ranking misses more than a quarter of independently verified relevant companies outright (Section~\ref{subsec:production_divergence_result}). Further experiments tuned and fine-tuned the pipeline against this silver standard, and found genuine, if uneven, gains: a small neural network fusion ranker that outperformed the tuned tree ensemble at every cutoff (Section~\ref{subsec:neural_fusion_ranker_result}), and a properly-trained fine-tuned reranker that beat its pretrained starting point at deeper cutoffs once its training signal was corrected (Section~\ref{subsec:reranker_finetuning_result}).

On efficiency, the accuracy--efficiency trade-off central to this thesis's research question was tested twice: first on a synthetic 2-million-vector corpus early on (Section~\ref{sec:ann_scaling}), and again on a real, nested corpus of up to 397,025 companies built specifically to test scaling (Section~\ref{sec:scaling_corpus}). Both experiments reach the same conclusion: approximate nearest-neighbour search matches exact search's retrieval quality within a few percentage points while running tens of times faster, and this advantage grows, not shrinks, as the corpus scales further (Section~\ref{subsec:ann_tuning_result}), directly supporting the efficiency half of this thesis's research question.

Read honestly and without overclaiming (Section~\ref{subsec:honest_synthesis}), this thesis demonstrates that its methodology is more rigorous than trusting a production system's own output as ground truth, that concrete, measurable improvements to the retrieval pipeline exist and were found, and that approximate search keeps the resulting pipeline efficient at meaningfully larger scale than the original evaluation corpus. A final production-independent test (Section~\ref{subsec:production_independent_eval_result}), evaluated exclusively on companies production had no opportunity to already know about, substantially closes the single most important claim this thesis had left open: at the full 397,025-company tested scale, the pipeline recovers 88.5\% of independently, blindly verified relevant companies, higher than this thesis's original headline Recall@1{,}000 figure (0.823). This is not, strictly, the symmetric head-to-head comparison against production's own ranking that Section~\ref{subsec:goi_vs_ours_ranking} set out to make, no such comparison is definable for a candidate pool production never ranked at all, but it is the strongest available evidence that the coverage gains claimed throughout this thesis (Section~\ref{subsec:production_divergence_result}) hold up once the pooling bias favouring production is removed by construction.

\section{Future Work}

Several concrete extensions were identified over the course of this thesis but not implemented, either for lack of time or because they depend on results not yet available. They are grouped here by what they would establish.

\paragraph{Closing the central open question.} The single most important claim this thesis had left open was whether its retrieval pipeline finds relevant companies production never surfaced, tested fairly rather than against a candidate pool partly pooled from production's own output. Section~\ref{subsec:production_independent_eval_result} now runs exactly this test, on the random, production-independent 300,000-company sample, and finds strong recall (0.885 at full scale) on a pool clean of that bias by construction, substantially, if not completely, closing the gap. Two smaller pieces of this remain: 99 of 1{,}229 judged candidates are still in the manual tie-break queue and had not been resolved at the time of writing, so the 695-company confirmed-relevant count used throughout Section~\ref{subsec:production_independent_eval_result} is a lower bound that could grow further; and, separately, re-scoring the original baseline embedding models' already-computed rankings against the current, non-circular silver standard, at no additional retrieval or encoding cost, would check whether the original baseline comparison (Section~\ref{sec:extended_baselines_result}) survives independent verification the same way the fusion ranker's own feature-importance finding was checked and found to partially reverse (Section~\ref{subsec:refit_fusion_ranker_result}).

\paragraph{Strengthening the evaluation methodology itself.} Precision figures throughout this thesis are conservative lower bounds rather than exact values, since the silver standard is pooled rather than exhaustive (Section~\ref{subsec:honest_synthesis}); a more exhaustive LLM ranking pass over an already-pooled candidate set, rather than the smaller actively-sampled subset judged here, would tighten this bound. Paired significance testing (Wilcoxon signed-rank and paired bootstrap confidence intervals, Notebook 71) has now been added for the three comparisons in this thesis where a directional claim was most load-bearing, tuned vs.\ untuned fusion ranker, neural network vs.\ tree ensemble, and ANN vs.\ exact search, and the results were not uniformly confirmatory: the hyperparameter-tuning trade-off did not reach significance on the 19-query leakage-free sample, the neural network's broad-list (NDCG@1{,}000) gain was confirmed but its top-of-list (NDCG@10) gain was not, and ANN's near-equivalence to exact search was confirmed directly (Sections~\ref{subsec:hyperparameter_tuning_result}, \ref{subsec:neural_fusion_ranker_result}, \ref{subsec:ann_tuning_result}). Extending the same testing to the remaining comparisons not yet covered (fine-tuned vs.\ pretrained reranker, production vs.\ independent ranking) is a direct next step using the same per-query data and methodology. The classifier's predicted probabilities are only moderately well calibrated, with a systematic gap in the low-to-middle confidence range (Section~\ref{subsec:calibration_check_result}); probability recalibration, for example Platt scaling or isotonic regression, before applying the fixed 0.5 relevance threshold used throughout is a direct, well-understood fix. Label propagation from judged candidates to their nearest, unjudged neighbours in embedding space (Section~\ref{subsec:active_learning}) remains a cheaper, complementary way to grow labelled data that was identified but not implemented. The joint relevance-label review with Istari's Kerem Cerit and Manav (Section~\ref{subsec:external_review_prep}) has since returned, but only as the lighter, curated 100-candidate export for two queries rather than the full ranked list originally planned, and with a single label column rather than one per reviewer, so neither the intended LLM-human nor human-human kappa could be computed from it (Section~\ref{subsec:external_review_result}). A genuinely blind, non-circular human check on this thesis's own silver labels was obtained a different way instead. A self-labelling exercise over a stratified 300-candidate sample, blind to the existing silver labels, reached a raw agreement of 78.7\% and a kappa of 0.603 (Section~\ref{subsec:pooled_gold_standard_result}). Closing the same gap through the external review remains open, and would need a fuller, higher-overlap sample from Kerem and Manav to compute a genuine human-human figure alongside it.

\paragraph{Further modelling experiments.} The neural network fusion ranker (Section~\ref{subsec:neural_fusion_ranker}) used a random hyperparameter search over a single train/validation split rather than the fully cross-validated search used for the tree ensemble; Bayesian optimisation, mentioned as a candidate strategy but not implemented for lack of a suitable dependency, could search the same space more efficiently and with a proper nested cross-validated estimate of its final performance. Fine-tuning was applied to the reranker (Section~\ref{subsec:reranker_finetuning}) but not to a bi-encoder embedding model directly, a larger and riskier undertaking given the modest size of the current silver standard, but one with real potential to improve first-stage retrieval quality rather than only reranking. The boilerplate/platform-summary contamination identified in the underlying corpus (Section~\ref{sec:trust_filter_result}) was quantified but not corrected in any result reported here; using the boilerplate-similarity score as an input feature to a trained model, rather than as a hard filter, would let the model learn how much to trust an imperfect signal instead of requiring it to be reliable enough for a fixed decision rule.

\paragraph{Scaling further.} Every experiment in this thesis operates on a corpus of at most 397,025 companies, roughly 2\% of Istari's full 20-million-company database. The ANN scaling results (Sections~\ref{sec:ann_scaling_result}, \ref{subsec:ann_tuning_result}) are consistent across a synthetic 2-million-vector test and a real 397,025-company test, and both point toward the same favourable trade-off, but neither directly measures behaviour at the full production scale. Testing whether retrieval quality, ANN's efficiency advantage, and the silver-standard construction pipeline itself all continue to hold at that scale would require a substantially larger data export from Istari than used in this thesis, and is the natural next step toward a production deployment of the methods developed here.
